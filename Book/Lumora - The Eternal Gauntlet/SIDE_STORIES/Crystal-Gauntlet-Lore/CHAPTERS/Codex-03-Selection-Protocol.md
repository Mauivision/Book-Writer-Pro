## Codex 03 — Selection Protocol

Haruto waited an hour before opening the codex again.

That was his compromise with fear.

He told himself that if he could delay it, he was still in control.

The cyan tab didn’t punish him for the delay.

It didn’t nag.

It didn’t flash.

It just sat there—quiet, polite, and absolutely certain it would win eventually.

When he finally selected it, he did it with the resignation of a man clicking “View Details” on a warning he knows he should not read in public.

The world blinked.

This time, the codex did not bring him to a workshop.

It brought him to a doorway.

Not a door in a wall.

A door in *the air*—a rectangular frame drawn in cyan lines, hovering in darkness like a UI element that had been made physical.

Beyond it, a hall stretched into nowhere.

The hall was lined with pillars.

And on each pillar, a cuff.

Haruto’s heart kicked.

It was instinctive, immediate, and irrationally possessive, like seeing your own name on a stranger’s lips.

There were dozens.

Hundreds.

Each cuff different in detail—some thicker, some thinner, some shaped like elegant bracelets, some like brutal manacles.

All of them threaded with cyan veins.

All of them waiting.

His wrist burned under the one he wore, and the burn made a statement his brain didn’t like:

*Mine.*

The codex answered that thought by being merciless.

> **ERROR**  
> Unique instance required.  
> This is a memory of a *rejected design set*.

Haruto swallowed.

So the Architects had tried more than once.

They had failed more than once.

The hall shifted like a loading screen skipping.

The cuffs on the pillars dissolved into lines and became diagrams—floating overlays that labeled failures without emotion.

> **Prototype A** — binds to bloodline. **Rejected:** bloodline corrupts.  
> **Prototype B** — binds to “worthiness.” **Rejected:** worthiness is narrative bias.  
> **Prototype C** — binds to “power.” **Rejected:** power attracts factions.  
> **Prototype D** — binds to “virtue.” **Rejected:** virtue is performative under stress.

Haruto exhaled slowly.

This wasn’t mythology.

This was quality assurance.

The codex did not care about heroes.

It cared about failure modes.

The hall collapsed into a single pillar.

On it hovered a cuff that looked almost identical to the one on Haruto’s wrist—crystal-lattice held by brass geometry, cyan veins pulsing softly like a living circuit.

An overlay formed around it, more complex than the others.

Not a label.

A protocol.

> **SELECTION PROTOCOL**  
> Objective: restore equilibrium during tilt events.  
> Constraint: selection must minimize systemic bias.  
> Constraint: selection must resist faction capture.  
> Constraint: selection must survive hostility from the world it repairs.

Haruto’s throat tightened.

“Hostility,” he thought, and his mind supplied the dungeon’s smirking malice.

The codex continued.

> **Candidate requirements:**  
> - Not bound to any local faction by birth or oath.  
> - Not invested in existing narratives of Light/Shadow.  
> - Capable of applying operations without worship.  
> - Disposable.

He choked on the last word.

Disposable.

Like a tool.

Like a battery.

Like a temporary patch.

Haruto felt his stomach turn, and for a second he was certain he would vomit in whatever real hallway his body was standing in.

The codex did not slow down for him.

It never had.

> **TERMINOLOGY NOTE**  
> Outsiders are not chosen because they are special.  
> Outsiders are chosen because they are *unclaimed*.

The hall changed again.

Now he saw a map of Lumora—raw, early, still glowing like heated metal. Cyan seams traced beneath continents. Light and shadow zones were not “factions” yet; they were gradients in the planet’s own behavior.

Above the map, a list of local candidate sets appeared.

Haruto recognized the shape of it.

It was a matchmaking algorithm.

The codex ran through local profiles like a scrolling feed.

**Candidate: Verdant artisan.** Rejected: oath-bonded to grove.  
**Candidate: Veil assassin.** Rejected: blood debt.  
**Candidate: Light cleric.** Rejected: doctrine loop.  
**Candidate: Sky-born raider.** Rejected: faction capture probability high.

Haruto’s eyes tracked the rejections with a rising, bitter understanding.

It wasn’t “fate” that made him the host.

It was that everyone else was already owned.

Then the codex showed him something that made his skin go cold.

It displayed a small, simple line of text.

> **Candidate: Outsider.**  
> Status: Unclaimed.  
> Status: Untrained.  
> Status: *Likely to ask why.*

Haruto’s laugh came out wrong.

Short.

Sharp.

“That’s not a qualification,” he wanted to argue.

The codex responded by showing him the next layer of the protocol.

> **Bias avoidance**  
> Outsider minds carry foreign assumptions.  
> Foreign assumptions resist local propaganda.  
> Resistance improves equilibrium outcomes.

The world blinked around the text and became a sequence of scenes—fast, brutal flashes, like someone scrubbing through a video timeline.

Haruto, in the dungeon, refusing to accept a “trial” as moral judgment.

Haruto, arguing with Sunny about why an NPC’s pain shouldn’t be treated as flavor text.

Haruto, forcing his party to negotiate with someone they’d been trained to hate because the System had labeled them “enemy.”

Each flash ended with a faint cyan overlay: **EQUILIBRIUM DELTA +**.

Haruto’s mouth went dry again.

He’d thought he was being clever.

He’d thought he was being human.

He’d been… performing as designed.

The codex offered another line, almost gentle in its cruelty.

> **Your refusal to worship the rules is why you were selected to enforce them.**

The map returned.

This time, icons appeared across Lumora—nodes, pathways, faction boundaries.

And then something else:

A set of lines that connected the boundaries to each other, bridging them like faint, ghostly roads.

They glowed cyan.

Haruto recognized the shape immediately because he’d seen it in the System UI.

Party slots.

Recruitment links.

The ability to invite people who “shouldn’t” be able to cooperate.

The codex labeled it with a term that hit like a punch.

> **CROSS-FACTION RECRUITMENT ENABLEMENT**  
> User-facing term: **Party Leader Authority**  
> Internal term: **Equilibrium Bridging**

Haruto’s pulse spiked.

So it wasn’t a skill.

It was infrastructure.

Not “leadership” as a personality trait.

Leadership as an engineered loophole the world used when it was desperate.

The codex showed him the failure mode.

A local leader gets chosen.

The leader recruits only their own.

The faction tightens.

The tilt worsens.

War.

Collapse.

Then an outsider gets chosen.

The outsider recruits across boundaries because the outsider doesn’t see those boundaries as sacred.

The tilt slows.

Not peace.

Motion.

Balance.

Haruto felt a strange, sour relief in his chest—relief that he wasn’t “destined,” relief that he wasn’t “the chosen one,” relief that this wasn’t about his soul being uniquely special.

And then the codex punished that relief by showing him the cost.

The hall shifted.

The pillars returned, but now each pillar held a silhouette instead of a cuff.

Outsiders.

Different shapes, different species, different eras. Some wore armor. Some wore robes. Some wore nothing but the haunted look of someone who had learned too much too quickly.

Each silhouette had a cyan line beneath it, like a status bar.

Each line was empty.

Haruto’s throat closed.

The codex displayed one final procedure.

> **HOST LIFE CYCLE**  
> Selection → Binding → Bridging → Tilt correction → Release  
> *Release method varies.*

He stared.

Release.

Like an app uninstall.

Like a service contract ending.

Like a hand being removed from a tool.

“Release method varies,” he read, and he hated that the codex wouldn’t specify.

He hated that it could be death.

He hated that it could be something worse.

The codex did not give him comfort.

It gave him context.

> **Protocol truth:**  
> The world is not obligated to keep you.  
> The world is obligated to keep itself.

The vision snapped shut.

Stone ceiling.

Dungeon hum.

The sound of someone shouting his name—distant, urgent, real.

Haruto blinked fast, trying to force tears back into his eyes before anyone could see them, because crying in a dungeon felt like signing a contract with whatever was watching.

His wrist throbbed under the cuff.

The cyan tab pulsed again.

Not like a heartbeat.

Like a reminder:

*You asked how the world works.*

Haruto swallowed hard.

“Yeah,” he whispered, and it came out rough. “I’m learning.”
