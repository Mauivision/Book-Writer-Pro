## Codex 20 — Bridge Tax

Haruto would have preferred a bill.

Something clear.

Something you could argue with.

Something you could pay and be done.

But Lumora didn’t charge you like a merchant.

Lumora charged you like weather.

Quietly.

Constantly.

And only obvious once you were already soaked.

The cyan tab pulsed.

Haruto stared at it with the exhausted resentment of a man who had realized the tutorial was also the debt collector.

He opened it.

The world blinked.

He stood on a bridge.

Not a stone bridge.

Not a Radiant light-bridge.

Not a Veil shadow-anchor.

A conceptual bridge—two sides of a chasm held together by a thin cyan lattice that hummed like a violin string under tension.

On one side, the air glowed gold.

On the other, violet.

Between them, the cyan lattice held.

And beneath it, the chasm was filled with moving lines—error states, old grudges, doctrines, debts—everything the world classified as “separation.”

The codex text appeared in cyan.

> **BRIDGE TAX**  
> Bridging is permitted.  
> Bridging is not free.

Haruto’s throat tightened.

He looked down and saw something etched into the cyan lattice.

Not decorative.

Numbers.

Meters.

A ledger.

The bridge kept a tally.

The codex highlighted the categories with calm cruelty:

> **COSTS (recurring):**  
> - Attention (witness keys)  
> - Inconvenience (micro-incidents)  
> - Exhaustion (host stability)  
> - Scarring (anchor burn)  
> - Drift (identity contamination)

Haruto swallowed.

Bridge tax wasn’t coins.

It was *life.*

The bridge shivered, and Haruto watched a scene play out across it like a reflection.

Haruto’s party walking together through a city street.

Radiant guard staring too long.

Veil courier shifting their cloak.

Rumors beginning like sparks.

The cyan lattice between them brightened as unity held.

Then the cost hit.

Not a Sentinel.

Smaller.

A cart wheel snapped.

A vendor’s tray tipped.

A child tripped into Seraphina’s path and Seraphina caught them—heroic, gentle—

and the crowd’s attention spiked.

Witness keys flared.

The bridge tax meter ticked up.

Haruto’s stomach twisted.

Every act of care was a toll booth.

The codex drew a thin cyan line from the bridge to a pale green node in the distance.

Verdant.

Anchor.

The line pulsed.

The codex displayed a note beneath it:

> **Anchor absorbs no cost.**  
> Anchor routes cost into survivable channels.

Routes.

Haruto thought of Elara.

Of Velvet’s steady hands.

Of Mira’s ability to redirect a rumor like a river.

The codex wasn’t romantic about anchors.

It was practical.

It showed him the interest rate.

The bridge tax meter ticked again.

This time, the category highlighted wasn’t attention.

It was scarring.

Haruto saw the anchor’s forearm glow with that bruised green-cyan staining.

Not fatal.

Not glamorous.

Persistent.

The codex’s line appeared like a warning label you couldn’t peel off.

> **Tax accumulates.**  
> The world prefers slow payment to sudden debt.

Haruto’s chest tightened.

So the world didn’t want him to die immediately.

It wanted him to pay slowly.

In micro-incidents.

In exhaustion.

In social consequences.

In little burns on the people who held his wrist steady.

Haruto’s cuff warmed.

The bridge lattice responded, humming louder, as if it recognized the host and updated the ledger.

The codex displayed the final note in small cyan text.

> **If you stop bridging, the tax stops.**  
> If you keep bridging, the tax becomes your climate.

The vision snapped shut.

Haruto blinked into the real world with the uncomfortable certainty that he could feel the tolls even when he couldn’t see them.

He could feel them in the way people stared a beat too long.

In the way laughter sharpened.

In the way his cuff warmed at the edge of a decision like it was checking a budget.

Bridge tax wasn’t a punishment.

It was the world’s price for allowing motion between enemies.

And Haruto, because he was stubborn and human and sick of being bought—

realized that the only way to pay less was to stop crossing.

Which meant the only way to save anyone was to keep paying anyway.
