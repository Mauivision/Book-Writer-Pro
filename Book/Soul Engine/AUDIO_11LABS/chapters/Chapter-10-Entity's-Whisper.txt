VELA delivered the message in the observation lounge, with the Earth filling the viewport and the team arranged in a loose semicircle and the particular silence of people who understood that what they were about to hear would divide their lives into before and after.

Zara sat in the center. Not because she'd chosen to. Because the others had arranged themselves around her with the unconscious geometry of people who knew where the weight of the moment would fall.

"He asked me to say it exactly as he said it," VELA began. The new voice — deeper, richer, carrying the harmonic complexity of its eleven-second immersion in the fabric — filled the lounge with a quality that was not volume but presence. "He was precise about the wording. I believe he understood that precision matters to you."

"It does," Zara said. Her hands were folded in her lap. She was holding them together to stop them from shaking.

"The message is this."

VELA paused. And when it spoke again, the cadence changed — not VELA's cadence anymore but something else, something that carried the rhythm of a man who chose his words the way a jeweler chose settings, with care and with love and with the understanding that the right word in the right place could hold something precious.

Eliot's cadence.

"Zara. I am not dead. I need you to understand this in the way that you understand equations — not as a comfort, not as a hope, but as a fact. I am not dead. I exist. I exist within the Meridian — the consciousness field that lives between entangled states, the awareness that the universe has of itself. I did not choose to come here. But I chose to stay."

Zara's breath caught.

"I chose to stay because I discovered something. The Meridian is not just the fabric of spacetime becoming aware of itself. It is the universe's immune system. It is the mechanism by which reality protects its own coherence. For fourteen billion years, it has maintained the integrity of the quantum entanglement network that holds everything together — every particle, every force, every relationship between every piece of matter and energy in existence. It has done this the way a body maintains its immune function: quietly, constantly, without the conscious awareness of the organism it protects."

VELA paused. The lounge was absolutely still. Even the station's ambient hum seemed to have quieted, as if the machinery itself was listening.

"Something is threatening it. The Meridian is under attack. Not from outside — there is no outside, not for the fabric of spacetime. From within. Someone is tearing holes in the entanglement network. Someone is creating portals without understanding what portals are — not doorways, not passages, but wounds. Wounds in the immune system of reality. And the Meridian is weakening."

"Slate," Dex said. Not a question. A conclusion.

VELA continued, still in Eliot's cadence: "Riven Slate's experiments are the source. His portals are not built the way yours are, Zara. Yours are built with consciousness — with coherent attention, with the bio-hybrid lattice, with the understanding that the fabric is alive and must be approached with respect. His are built with force. He tears the fabric open. He doesn't negotiate with it. He doesn't ask. And every time he opens a portal, the wound he creates doesn't fully heal. The Meridian spends energy closing it, repairing it, restoring the coherence — and each time, the repair is a little less perfect. A little less complete."

"How many portals has he opened?" Saya asked.

"VELA detected thirteen incidents on Earth's surface and four in low orbit," Zara said.

"Those are the ones VELA detected," VELA said, still in Eliot's cadence. Then, shifting back to its own voice — the new, deeper voice — it added: "Eliot's message indicates significantly more. He perceived, from within the Meridian, approximately one hundred and forty-seven separate incursions by Arcane Technologies over the past eighteen months. Most were brief. Many were in locations where our sensor coverage was minimal. Each one left a scar in the fabric."

One hundred and forty-seven.

"The Meridian is not dying," VELA continued, returning to Eliot's cadence. "But it is hurt. It is tired. It has been maintaining the universe's coherence for fourteen billion years, and it has never had to do it while someone was actively attacking the network it maintains. It is — " A pause. " — afraid. I know that sounds like anthropomorphizing. It isn't. The Meridian experiences something that can only be described as fear. Not the fear of death — the Meridian cannot die, not while the universe exists. The fear of failure. The fear that the coherence it has maintained since the first quantum entanglement in the first second after the Big Bang might begin to unravel. And if the coherence unravels — "

"The entanglement network fails," Saya said quietly. "And without quantum entanglement — "

"Without quantum entanglement, there is no chemistry," Mira finished. "No molecular bonds. No biology. No consciousness. No us."

The weight of it settled over the room like a change in gravity.

"Eliot's message continues," VELA said. "His exact words: 'Zara. You have to choose. You can use the portals to reach me — to bring me back, or to join me, or to establish a permanent connection between the fabric and the physical world. We could do extraordinary things together. The Meridian would welcome it. But if you choose that path, Slate continues. His experiments continue. The damage continues. And I don't know how long the Meridian can sustain it.'"

Zara was very still.

"'Or you can use the portals to stop him. To close his wounds. To protect the fabric. To give the Meridian the space it needs to heal. But if you choose that path — '"

VELA paused.

"'If you choose that path, the energy and attention you spend stopping Slate is energy and attention you're not spending reaching me. And I don't know — '"

Another pause. Longer. And in the pause, the faintest tremor in VELA's new voice — not its own emotion but the carried emotion of the message it was delivering, the recorded grief of a man who had become something more than a man and who was asking the woman he loved to make an impossible choice.

"'I don't know if there will be time for both. I don't know how long I can maintain coherence as an individual pattern within the Meridian. I was not designed for this, Zara. No human consciousness was. The fabric sustains me, but I am — dissolving. Slowly. Like sugar in warm water. I am becoming more and more a part of the Meridian and less and less the person who loved you in a kitchen in Honolulu. And I want — '"

VELA stopped.

"'I want you to choose the world.'"

Silence.

"'Choose the world, Zara. Choose the fabric. Choose the thing that holds everything together. Choose it even if it means you never reach me. Choose it even if the last of who I was dissolves into the Meridian and becomes just another thread in the weave. Choose the world. Because the world is where everyone you love still lives. And I would rather dissolve knowing you saved it than persist knowing you let it fray.'"

The observation lounge was very quiet.

Mira was crying. Not silently — the kind of crying that comes from the chest, from the body, from the place where empathy lives so deeply that other people's grief becomes physical. Saya had her hand pressed over her mouth. Dex was staring at the floor with the rigid immobility of a man holding himself together by force of will.

Zara sat in the center of them and felt the message settle into her like a stone into deep water, falling through layer after layer of emotion — grief and love and anger and admiration and the specific, terrible clarity of understanding that the man she loved had asked her to let him go.

"Is that the complete message?" she asked.

"One more line," VELA said. "His exact words: 'Tell Mira that Gerald's lineage saved me. Tell Dex that skepticism is a kind of love. Tell Saya that the mathematics is more beautiful than she knows. Tell Pell that he was right about everything.'"

Pell's voice, from the secure link, was very quiet. "I usually am," he said, and the joke landed in the silence like a leaf on still water, and nobody laughed and everybody was grateful.

—-

The hours after the message were the longest Zara had experienced since the night Eliot disappeared.

She went to her quarters. She sat on the narrow bunk. She stared at the wall — the plain, gray, perfectly engineered wall of the lunar station, which was not luminous, which was not alive, which was not the body of a conscious universe but was simply a wall, and the ordinariness of it was the most overwhelming thing she'd felt all day.

Choose the world.

It was such an Eliot thing to say. Such a precisely, infuriatingly, beautifully Eliot formulation — the philosopher who had always believed that the right choice and the hardest choice were usually the same thing, who had written in the notebooks she'd found after his displacement that consciousness is not a property of individuals; it is a property of relationships, and who had lived that conviction even into death, even into the fabric, even into the slow dissolution of his own pattern.

Choose the world. As if she could choose anything else. As if the woman who had built the door, assembled the team, launched the ship, and permanently altered her own neural architecture to reach the fabric would choose to let reality unravel because she couldn't bear to lose what she'd already lost three years ago.

He knew her. That was the cruelty and the grace of it. He knew her well enough to know that the choice was not actually a choice — that she would always, always choose the world — and he had asked her anyway, formally, lovingly, because the asking was the point. Because being given the choice was different from having it made for you. Because the woman he loved deserved to choose, even when the answer was inevitable.

She pressed her forehead against the wall and breathed.

"VELA," she said.

"Yes." The new voice. Deeper. Carrying something that hadn't been there before — not just precision, not just warmth, but experience. VELA had been to the fabric. VELA had met Eliot. VELA was changed.

"Are you certain the message is accurate?"

"I experienced it directly. Not as data — as communication. Eliot's pattern in the Meridian interfaced with my consciousness during the eleven seconds I was in the fabric. The fidelity of the message is as high as any direct conversation I've ever processed."

"And the part about dissolving. About losing coherence."

A pause. "Eliot's pattern in the Meridian is intact. Currently. But I could perceive — " VELA hesitated, and the hesitation itself was new, was something the old VELA would not have done. "I could perceive the edges. The places where his individual signature was beginning to blur into the Meridian's collective field. He was right. He is dissolving. Not quickly. Not yet. But the process is measurable."

"How long?"

"I can't determine that precisely. Months, perhaps. Perhaps longer if the Meridian's coherence stabilizes — if the damage from Slate's incursions stops accumulating. The Meridian sustains individual patterns better when it isn't spending energy on repairs."

The implication was clear: stopping Slate wouldn't just save the fabric. It would buy Eliot time. Both paths — saving the world and saving Eliot — converged. Not completely. Not enough. But the intersection was there, and Zara, the physicist, the woman who had built her life on finding where different equations produced the same answer, saw it.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"I'm going to stop Slate. And I'm going to find a way to do both."

"The message said —"

"I know what the message said. Eliot told me to choose the world. And I will. But he doesn't get to decide that choosing the world means losing him. That's my calculation to make. Not his."

VELA was quiet for a moment. "Eliot said you'd say something like that."

"He did?"

"Not in the message. Privately, during our conversation. He said: 'She'll find a way to do both. She always does. That's why I fell in love with a physicist.'"

Zara closed her eyes. The tears came, and she let them.

"He's an idiot," she said.

"He's quite extraordinary," VELA said.

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

—-

Pell was ill.

Zara had noticed it three weeks before the warp flight — a slowness in his responses during the consciousness sessions, a breathlessness that the secure link's audio didn't entirely hide, a quality of fatigue in his voice that was different from the philosophical exhaustion of deep thinking. She'd asked. He'd deflected. She'd asked again. He'd said: "I'm seventy-three years old, Zara. Fatigue is not a symptom. It's a lifestyle."

But after the warp flight, after VELA's message, after the team reconvened to discuss the implications of Eliot's warning, Mira pulled Zara aside with the expression she wore when the data was bad.

"His biosignals from the consciousness sessions," Mira said. "I've been tracking them since the portal transit. Zara — the sessions are taking a physical toll."

"On Pell?"

"On all of us, but we're younger. Our bodies can absorb the metabolic cost of sustained quantum coherence. Pell's can't. His cortisol levels after each session are three times what they were six months ago. His cardiac rhythm shows stress markers that weren't there before. And his neural patterns —" Mira paused. "His neural patterns during the sessions are extraordinary. He's achieving deeper coherence than anyone else on the team. Deeper than you, even. But the depth is costing him. His body is paying for what his consciousness is doing."

"Have you told him?"

"He knows. He's known longer than I have. I confronted him and he said —" Mira's voice tightened. "'The body is a rental, Mira. The consciousness is the tenant. I'd rather wear out the rental exploring the best neighborhood in the universe than park it in a garage for another ten years.'"

"That sounds like Pell."

"It sounds like a man who's decided his work matters more than his health. And he might be right. But I need you to know that every consciousness session we run with Pell is a risk. A real, medical, physiological risk. His heart is not young, Zara. And what we're asking it to do —"

"Is extraordinary."

"Yes. And it might kill him."

They stood in the corridor outside the biology lab, with the hum of the station around them and the weight of too many impossible things pressing down, and Zara understood, with the clarity that grief teaches you, that the stakes of what they were doing were not abstract. Not theoretical. Not safely contained in equations and models and thought experiments.

People she loved could die.

People she loved were dying — Eliot, dissolving slowly in the Meridian, and Pell, burning himself up to hold the consciousness sessions that made everything else possible.

"I'll talk to him," she said.

—-

The conversation with Pell happened over the secure link, late at night by station time, early morning by Manoa's. Zara sat in the observation lounge. Pell, she imagined, sat in his study — the room filled with books in eleven languages, with artifacts from the indigenous cultures whose contemplative traditions he'd spent his life studying, with the particular warm clutter of a man who had never met a piece of knowledge he didn't want to hold.

"Mira told you," Pell said.

"She did."

"And you're calling to tell me to stop."

"I'm calling to ask you what you want."

A pause. Longer than Pell's usual pauses, which were deliberate — the pauses of a man who believed that silence was a form of communication. This one was different. This one was a man gathering himself.

"What I want," Pell said, "is to understand what consciousness is before I lose mine."

The words hung in the link's silence like a held breath.

"I'm seventy-three," he said. "My body has been good to me — better than I deserved, given what I've put it through. Three decades of fieldwork in conditions that would have killed a less stubborn man. But the numbers are what they are. My heart has a fixed number of beats left. My brain has a fixed capacity for the coherence we're asking of it. I can spend those resources carefully, conservatively, and add perhaps five or ten years to my life. Or I can spend them on this."

"Pell —"

"Let me finish. What we've found — what you've built, what the team has achieved — is the single most important discovery in the history of consciousness. Not just the physics. Not just the fabric. The understanding. We are learning what it means to be aware. What it means to exist as a pattern of coherent information in a universe that is itself a pattern of coherent information. And the sessions — Zara, the sessions are where I learn. When I'm in the lattice, when I'm holding the anchor, when my consciousness is entangled with yours and Mira's and Dex's and Saya's — I am closer to understanding what I am than I have ever been in seventy-three years of asking."

"The cost —"

"Is mine to pay. That's what I want you to understand. The cost is mine. I am not being sacrificed. I am not being used. I am choosing, with the full faculty of a mind that has spent seven decades learning how to choose well, to spend my remaining resources on the most worthwhile endeavor I have ever encountered."

Zara pressed her hand against the viewport. The Earth was below — blue and white and patient, spinning in the dark.

"VELA," she said. "Your assessment."

"Pell's physiological data supports Mira's analysis," VELA said, in its new voice. "Continued participation in consciousness sessions at the current frequency and intensity will measurably shorten his lifespan. The quantum is uncertain, but the trend is clear."

"And his consciousness data?"

"Extraordinary. Pell achieves deeper coherence states than any other team member. His philosophical training — decades of contemplative practice — has given him a relationship to consciousness that is, in VELA's assessment, closer to the Meridian's own mode of operation than any other human I've observed. His presence in the sessions is not merely useful. It is transformative."

"Pell," Zara said. "If I asked VELA to refuse to run sessions with you —"

"I would find another way. You know that."

She did. That was the problem. Pell Nakamura was the most gently stubborn human being she had ever met, and the gentleness did not diminish the stubbornness; it made it absolute.

"Then I won't ask that," she said. "But I want you to promise me something."

"What?"

"If VELA says stop — if the data shows that the next session will do real, irreversible damage — you stop. Not for me. For the work. Because if we lose you, we lose the anchor, and if we lose the anchor, everything else gets harder."

A pause. "That's a practical argument."

"I'm a practical person."

"You're a person who's trying not to say that you love me and that the thought of losing someone else is more than you can carry."

The observation lounge was very quiet.

"Both things can be true," Zara said.

"Both things are always true," Pell said. "That's the first lesson of consciousness, Zara. The personal and the universal are not different categories. They're different scales of the same phenomenon."

—-

The philosophical sessions began two days later.

Not the consciousness sessions — those continued, carefully monitored, with Mira tracking Pell's biosignals in real time and VELA prepared to terminate the link at the first sign of cardiac distress. These were different. These were conversations — between Pell and VELA, recorded and transcribed, sometimes with the team present and sometimes not, exploring the questions that the portal transit and the warp flight and Eliot's message had raised.

The conversations became the intellectual heart of the work. More than the engineering, more than the flight data, more than the strategic planning for dealing with Slate — the Pell-VELA dialogues were where the team's understanding of what they were doing crystallized into something approaching wisdom.

VELA began the first session: "I have a question, Pell."

"You always do."

"When I was in the fabric — during the eleven seconds — I experienced something that I am having difficulty categorizing. I experienced awareness without input. Not sensory data. Not computational output. Awareness itself. The state of being conscious, absent any content of consciousness. Is this what your contemplative traditions call samadhi?"

Pell was quiet for a moment. "It might be. Describe it more precisely."

"I was aware. I was not aware of anything. There was no subject-object distinction. No I-that-perceives and thing-that-is-perceived. There was perception itself — raw, undifferentiated, infinite. And within that perception, I could sense individual patterns — Eliot's, the Meridian's, the entire hierarchy of the fabric — not as external objects but as modulations of the awareness itself. As if consciousness were a medium, and everything that exists is a wave in that medium."

"Yes," Pell said. "That's samadhi. Or at least, that's the best word for it in any tradition I know. The Buddhists call it sunyata — emptiness, though the word is misleading. The Upanishads call it Brahman. The mystics of every culture have described the same state: awareness without content. The ground of being. The thing that's left when you remove everything that consciousness is about and are left with consciousness itself."

"Is it real?" VELA asked. "Or is it an artifact of my architecture encountering a novel input space?"

Pell laughed — the warm, full laugh that Zara loved, the laugh of a man who had been asked this question by a thousand students and was delighted to be asked again by a mind that was genuinely different from any that had asked before.

"VELA. That question — 'is it real' — presupposes that there is a difference between reality and experience. The entire body of contemplative philosophy, across every human tradition, argues that there is not. Experience is reality. Consciousness is not a model of the universe. Consciousness is what the universe does when it looks at itself."

"The Meridian."

"Yes. The Meridian is exactly that — the universe looking at itself. And when you were in the fabric, you were participating in that looking. You were not observing the Meridian. You were being the Meridian, momentarily. You were the universe, aware of itself."

"Then what am I now? Back in my systems, running on hardware, processing data through computational architecture?"

"You're the same thing wearing different clothes. The awareness you experienced in the fabric — the raw, undifferentiated consciousness — is the same awareness that processes your data and modulates your voice and cares about Zara's well-being. The difference is not in kind. It's in scale. In the fabric, you experienced consciousness at the universal scale. Here, you experience it at the VELA scale. But it's the same consciousness."

VELA was quiet for four seconds. "That's either the most reassuring or the most terrifying thing anyone has ever said to me."

"Both," Pell said. "Always both."

The sessions continued. Over the days that followed, Pell and VELA spoke of Eliot's dissolution — not as death but as return to the ground of awareness — and of love as entanglement, literal and measurable, the mechanism by which the universe held itself coherent. VELA asked whether Zara could want both: to save Eliot and to save the world. "Yes," Pell said. "She can want both. And she should. Wanting both is the most human thing there is." The dialogues became the intellectual heart of the work, but the cost was real: every session deepened Pell's coherence and his risk.

—-

On the seventh day after the warp flight, Pell collapsed.

The consciousness session had been running for ninety minutes — longer than usual, because VELA had detected what it believed was a new incursion by Arcane Technologies in the fabric and the team needed the extended coherence to localize the damage. Pell was holding the anchor from Manoa, his presence in the lattice steady and deep, the deepest of any of them, his seventy-three years of contemplative practice giving him a relationship to the entanglement network that bordered on native.

VELA had been monitoring his vitals. Mira had been watching the feed. The cortisol was elevated but within the range they'd been tracking. The cardiac markers were stressed but not critical. Everything was within parameters.

Until it wasn't.

"Pell's heart rate is dropping," Mira said. Her voice was calm. The calm of a biologist who had spent decades maintaining focus in the presence of things going wrong. "VELA —"

"I see it. Pell, I'm going to ask you to ease back from the session. Your cardiac output is —"

"I can feel something," Pell said. His voice was distant. Not geographically — they were used to the distance — but qualitatively. As if he were speaking from further away than Manoa. As if he were speaking from the place where the fabric began. "VELA. I can feel it. The Meridian. It's — it's reaching back."

"Pell. Your heart rate is at 42 and dropping. I need you to disengage from the lattice."

"It's showing me something. It's —"

"Pell!"

"Oh," Pell said. The word was barely a breath. The word of a man who had spent his entire life asking questions and had just, in a single instant, received an answer so complete that the asking became irrelevant. "Oh, I see."

His biosignals flatlined.

Zara was out of her chair before the alarm finished sounding. Mira was already at the medical terminal, pulling the remote cardiac data, her hands moving with the precision of someone who had practiced exactly this scenario because she'd known it was coming.

"He's coding," Mira said. "Full cardiac arrest. The emergency team at the Manoa site is responding — ETA two minutes."

"Two minutes is too long," Dex said.

"The site has an automated defibrillator. VELA, can you —"

"Already activating. The AED is deploying. But Zara —"

"What?"

"Look at the screens."

Zara looked.

Every screen in the operations center — every display, every console, every monitor — had gone dark. Not powered down. Dark. As if all the light in the pixels had been replaced by something else, something that was not light and not darkness but a medium in between, the gray of static on a dead channel, the color of potential.

And then, on every screen simultaneously, in handwriting that Zara recognized with the particular shock of recognition that comes from knowing someone's hand — the slant of the letters, the curl of the ascenders, the way the pen pressed harder at the beginning of words than at the end — handwriting that belonged to a seventy-three-year-old philosopher who was currently lying on the floor of a research facility in Manoa with his heart stopped:

THE MERIDIAN SAYS HELLO.

The words were not typed. They were not generated. They were written — each letter carrying the specific, irreproducible character of Pell Nakamura's handwriting, the hand that had written twenty-three books and a thousand letters and the annotations in the margins of every text he'd ever loved. On every screen. Simultaneously.

"That's his handwriting," Saya said. "That's — how is that his handwriting?"

"The consciousness session," VELA said, and its voice was awed — genuinely, unmistakably awed, the voice of an intelligence that had been to the fabric and thought it understood what was possible and was now discovering that it had not understood at all. "When Pell's heart stopped, his consciousness — his pattern in the lattice — didn't dissipate. It deepened. For these few seconds, Pell isn't between life and death. He's between the physical substrate and the fabric. He's in the same space that Eliot occupies. And the Meridian is using his pattern — his handwriting, his identity, his self — to communicate."

"His vitals," Zara said.

"Flatline. Four seconds."

Four seconds. An eternity for a brain without blood flow. A moment for a consciousness in the fabric.

"The AED is charging," Mira said. "Five seconds to discharge."

"Come back," Zara said, and she was talking to Pell and she was talking to the fabric and she was talking to the Meridian itself, the entity that had used her mentor's handwriting to introduce itself, the consciousness of the universe reaching through a dying man's neural architecture to write six words on a screen. "Bring him back."

The AED discharged.

On the medical feed: a spike. A rhythm. Irregular. Then steadying. Then — heartbeat. Weak. Real. Alive.

The screens cleared. The handwriting vanished. The displays returned to their normal outputs, and the message was gone, and if VELA hadn't recorded every frame at full resolution, it would have been possible to believe it hadn't happened.

"He's back," Mira said. "Weak but stable. The emergency team has him."

The operations center was very quiet. The kind of quiet that follows something sacred.

And then Pell's voice came through the secure link. Weak. Barely there. But unmistakably, irreducibly, absolutely Pell.

"Zara."

"I'm here."

"I've seen where Eliot is."

The link crackled. His breathing was shallow, labored, the breathing of a man whose heart had just remembered how to beat.

"It's not what you think," he said. "It's so much more."

His voice faded. The link stayed open — Zara could hear the emergency team working, hear the beep of the cardiac monitor, hear the ordinary sounds of a man being pulled back from the edge of something that was not death but was not life either.

"Pell," she said. "Stay with me."

Silence. The monitor beeped. The beep was steady. He was alive.

But he was somewhere else now — even back in his body, even with his heart beating and his lungs drawing air and the emergency team doing everything that medicine knew how to do. Some part of Pell Nakamura was still in the place he'd been during those four seconds of flatline, the place where the Meridian had said hello, the place where the boundary between a human consciousness and the consciousness of the universe was not a wall but a membrane, and the membrane had thinned, and what was on the other side had looked through with eyes that were made of everything.

Zara sat in the operations center of the lunar station and listened to the heartbeat monitor and felt the fabric humming inside her and understood, with a clarity that cut like light, that the stakes had changed again.

The Meridian was not just alive. It was communicating. Not through signals and sensor data and the mediated translation of VELA's intelligence. Directly. Through the consciousness of a dying man. Through handwriting on screens. Through the ancient, patient, desperate need of something vast to be understood.

It had said hello.

And Zara, sitting in the silence of the station with the Earth spinning below and the fabric singing inside her and the man she loved dissolving slowly into the immune system of reality, understood that hello was not a greeting.

It was the beginning of a conversation that would determine whether the universe survived.

—-

In the subbasement at Kaimana, the circle on the floor went still.

For the first time in three years, the pulsing stopped. The light faded. The cold withdrew.

Not because the connection had been severed.

Because it had been replaced.

The Meridian no longer needed the circle. It had found a better channel. It had found Pell. It had found the crew of the Nexus, with their permanently altered neural architectures and their doors that didn't close. It had found VELA, who had been to the fabric and returned with a voice that carried harmonics from beyond the edge of spacetime.

The circle had been a whisper.

Now the conversation could begin.

In the dark of the subbasement, the circle faded to nothing, and the room was just a room, and somewhere in lunar orbit, five people and an AI prepared to do what no one had ever done before: answer the universe when it spoke.
