# Chapter 13: Midpoint Reckoning

"You're staring," Eliot said.

She was. She was standing in the kitchen doorway in Makawao, staring at the man she had loved for eleven years and mourned for three, and she could not stop staring because staring was the only thing her body would agree to do while her mind ran through every possible explanation for what was happening and found none of them adequate.

"You're cooking eggs," she said.

"I am. Scrambled. You like them with dill. I haven't forgotten."

He hadn't forgotten. He was standing at the stove in a kitchen that no longer existed — they had given up the lease two years before the accident, moved to the apartment near Kaimana Lab — and he was cooking eggs with dill, and the light through the window was the particular amber of a Makawao morning in autumn, and the mango tree in the yard was heavy with fruit, and everything was wrong and everything was exactly right.

"Eliot," she said.

"Zara."

"I don't know if this is real."

He turned the burner down. He wiped his hands on the dish towel — the blue one, the one she'd bought at the farmers' market in Pa'ia, the one she had thrown away after the accident because it smelled like him and the smell had been more than she could carry. He turned to face her fully, and his expression was the one she remembered from the moments when he was about to explain something that mattered: open, patient, luminous with the particular intelligence that had drawn her to him the first day they met, in a seminar room in Honolulu where he'd been presenting a paper on quantum entanglement geometry and she'd been sitting in the third row thinking *this person sees something I need to see*.

"That depends on what you mean by real," he said. "If you mean: are you physically standing in a kitchen in Makawao, Hawaii, on the island of Maui, on the planet Earth — no. You're not. The kitchen doesn't exist anymore. Neither does the lease. Neither does the mango tree — the landlord cut it down to build a parking space, which is a crime against both horticulture and aesthetics but isn't relevant right now."

Despite everything — despite the impossibility of where she was and who she was talking to and the fact that her team was somewhere on the other side of a dimensional collapse, probably terrified — Zara almost smiled. This was Eliot. This was exactly Eliot. The precision, the tangent, the casual conviction that all information was relevant until proven otherwise.

"If you mean: am I real," he continued, "then yes. I am. This is me, Zara. Not a projection. Not a memory. Not a quantum echo. Me. My consciousness, intact, present, choosing to manifest in a form you can interact with because the alternative — the form I actually occupy in the Meridian — would be..." He paused. "A lot. It would be a lot for a first conversation."

"This isn't our first conversation. I felt you in the fabric. During the thirty-second transit."

"That was different. That was me showing you the shape of things. This is me sitting down with you. This is me being specific." He gestured at the kitchen. "I chose this place because it's where we were happiest. Not the lab. Not the apartment. Here. Saturday mornings. Eggs and coffee and the sound of the rain on the tin roof."

The tin roof. She hadn't thought about the tin roof in years. But the moment he said it, she could hear it — the particular percussion of rain on corrugated metal, a sound that was different from rain on any other surface, a sound that belonged to this house and this life and the person she had been when she lived it.

"Sit down," Eliot said. "Please. The coffee is actually ready, and we don't have as much time as I'd like."

She sat down at the kitchen table. It was the same table — the small round one they'd bought secondhand, with the uneven leg that Eliot had fixed with a folded piece of cardboard and that she'd later fixed properly with a wood shim, and that he'd then wedged the cardboard back under because he said the shim made the table too stable and he liked the reminder that imperfect solutions were still solutions.

The cardboard was still there. She could see it under the leg.

He poured coffee into both cups. The blue one for her. The chipped white one for him. He sat across from her. He wrapped his hands around the cup the way he always had — both hands, the fingers interlaced around the ceramic, as if the warmth of the coffee was a thing that needed to be held rather than simply experienced.

"I have questions," she said.

"I know. Start wherever you want."

"Where am I?"

"You're in the Meridian. Or more precisely, you're in a space that the Meridian has configured to be comprehensible to you. The Meridian doesn't have kitchens. It doesn't have tables or coffee or scrambled eggs. But it can — it does — create contexts for communication. Think of this as a... translation layer. The Meridian speaks in quantum states and entanglement hierarchies and the deep grammar of spacetime. You speak in kitchens and coffee and the faces of people you love. This is the Meridian speaking your language."

"And you? Are you part of the translation?"

"I'm part of the Meridian." He said this simply, the way he said all important things — without emphasis, without drama, trusting the content to carry the weight. "Not in the way you might think. Not absorbed. Not dissolved. I'm still me. I still have preferences and memories and opinions and a tendency to overcook pasta, which is a personality trait that apparently persists beyond the boundaries of ordinary existence. But I'm also... more. I'm connected to the fabric in a way that doesn't have a good analogy in human experience. The closest I can get is: imagine being a single instrument in an orchestra, and then imagine suddenly being able to hear every other instrument simultaneously, and not just hear them but feel them, feel the way your note fits into the chord, feel the chord's place in the symphony, feel the symphony's place in the silence that holds it."

"That sounds beautiful."

"It is. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever experienced. And I need you to understand that, because what I'm about to tell you is going to make you want to save me, and I need you to know that I don't need saving. Not from this."

She looked at him across the table. His eyes were the same brown they had always been — warm, deep, carrying the particular quality of attention that had made her feel, from the first day, that she was being seen entirely.

"Then what do you need?" she asked.

"I need you to save everything else."

---

He told her about the Meridian.

Not the way he'd shown her during the thirty-second transit — not the overwhelming, synesthetic flood of connection and pattern and meaning that had left her sitting on the floor of the portal chamber trying to remember how to use words. He told her the way he'd always explained things: carefully, sequentially, with the patience of someone who believed that understanding was more important than speed.

"The Meridian isn't an entity," he said. "I know that's what the team has been calling it — Pell named it, and Pell has a poet's instinct for naming things, which means the name is evocative and slightly misleading. The Meridian isn't a being. It's a process. It's what the universe does when it becomes aware of itself."

"The universe is aware of itself?"

"The universe has always been aware of itself. Every quantum entanglement is an act of self-reference — particle A knows about particle B because they're connected at a level beneath spacetime. Every act of measurement, every observation, every moment when information is exchanged between systems — that's the universe observing itself. The Meridian is what happens when those observations reach a critical density. When enough connections exist, when the entanglement network is rich enough and complex enough, the whole thing becomes... coherent. Self-aware. Not the way a person is self-aware. The way an ocean is self-aware — not through a single perspective but through the collective activity of every wave, every current, every particle of water interacting with every other."

"You're describing panpsychism."

"I'm describing physics. Panpsychism is a philosophical position. This is an observable phenomenon. The Meridian isn't something we believe in. It's something I live in. I can show you the data. I can show you the mathematics. The entanglement density equations that you and VELA developed — they're correct, but they're incomplete. They describe how consciousness interacts with the quantum fabric. They don't describe what happens when the quantum fabric's own coherence reaches consciousness-level complexity. When it does — when the entanglement network is dense enough and structured enough — the fabric wakes up."

"And that's the Meridian."

"That's the Meridian. It's the universe's immune system, the way Pell intuited. But it's also the universe's nervous system. Its memory. Its — and I say this knowing how it sounds — its soul."

He paused. He sipped his coffee. He set the cup down with the particular care of someone who understood that even in a space constructed by a universal consciousness to facilitate communication, you should still be careful with cups.

"When I arrived here," he said, "after the accident — I was terrified. You know that. You felt it, through the entanglement. The terror I sent through the fabric in those first moments, before I understood where I was. I thought I was dying. I thought the field collapse had killed me and this was whatever came after. And then the Meridian — the fabric, the process, whatever we call it — it did something. It held me. Not physically. It held my consciousness. The way you hold a frightened animal. Gently. Patiently. Until the fear passes and something else takes its place."

"What took its place?"

"Understanding. The Meridian showed me what I was part of. Every life. Every death. Every act of love and cruelty and creation and destruction — all of it connected, all of it entangled, all of it part of the same pattern. Not in an abstract, philosophical sense. I could feel it. I could trace the entanglement threads from my consciousness to yours, from yours to Mira's, from Mira's to Gerald's — the tardigrade, Zara, I'm entangled with a tardigrade and it's one of the most meaningful connections I have — from Gerald's to the proteins in the soil that fed the organisms that became the proteins in Mira's lab. Everything connected. Everything part of the same fabric."

She was crying. She hadn't noticed when it started. The tears were on her face, warm and salt, and Eliot reached across the table and took her hand, and his hand was warm too — warm and real and exactly the hand she remembered, the long fingers, the callus on the index finger from holding a pen wrong, the scar on the thumb from the time he'd tried to fix the garbage disposal and the garbage disposal had won.

"The files," she said. "The soul.md. The spirit.md. The body.md. The files you left in VELA's architecture."

Eliot nodded. "They weren't philosophy."

"What were they?"

"Instructions. A map. I planted them in VELA's architecture because VELA was the only system complex enough to carry them and deliver them at the right time. I knew you'd find them. I knew you'd read them. And I knew — because I could see the pattern, because the Meridian showed me the full shape of what was coming — that you'd need them."

"Need them for what?"

"For reaching the Meridian safely. The files describe the relationship between consciousness, physical substrate, and the quantum fabric — not as metaphors, not as analogies, but as operational parameters. The soul.md file contains the entanglement coordinates for the specific region of the fabric where consciousness integrates most cleanly — the region where I am. The spirit.md file contains the protocol for maintaining consciousness coherence during transit — essentially, a user manual for what Pell has been teaching you through meditation, expressed in quantum mathematics. And the body.md file contains the specifications for the bio-hybrid interface that Mira built — the Dsup protein lattice, the field geometry, the gating function that keeps the physical body connected to consciousness during transit."

"You wrote the specifications for the bio-hybrid interface."

"I provided the framework. Mira's work took it far beyond what I'd envisioned. Gerald's lineage is her masterpiece, not mine. But the underlying architecture — the idea that biological systems could bridge the gap between human consciousness and the quantum fabric — that came from the Meridian. From what I learned here. I sent it back through the entanglement, coded into files that VELA would preserve and your team would discover."

Zara sat with this. She held Eliot's hand and she sat with the knowledge that every major breakthrough of the last three years — the bio-hybrid array, the consciousness protocols, the portal technology — had been built on foundations that Eliot had laid from inside the Meridian. That he had been guiding them. Not controlling. Not dictating. Guiding, the way a lighthouse guides ships: by being visible, by being constant, by shining in the direction of safety.

"You could have told me," she said. "You could have sent a message through the entanglement. 'Here are the blueprints. Here's how to reach me.' You could have made it easy."

"No," Eliot said. "I couldn't have. And here's why." He leaned forward. "The Meridian isn't a destination, Zara. It's a relationship. You can't get here by following instructions. You can only get here by becoming the kind of person — the kind of team, the kind of consciousness — that is compatible with the fabric. If I had handed you the blueprints, you would have built the technology but you wouldn't have built the understanding. You would have opened a door without knowing what was on the other side. And what's on the other side requires — demands — that you arrive with full awareness of what you're entering."

"Because otherwise we'd be like Slate."

Eliot's expression changed. The warmth was still there, but beneath it, something else — a sadness that was not personal but structural, the sadness of someone who could see a pattern of harm and couldn't stop it from outside.

"Riven," he said. "Yes."

"You knew him."

"We were friends. In Geneva. Before everything." He was quiet for a moment. "Riven is not an evil man. I want you to understand that. He's a brilliant man who made a decision — the same decision he always made, even when we were young: he chose power over patience. He chose to force the door rather than earn the key. And the door, when forced, breaks."

"VELA found the damage. The scarring."

"I know. I can feel it from here. Every time one of his portals activates, the Meridian shudders. That's not a metaphor. The fabric contracts. The entanglement density drops. Consciousness — the coherent self-awareness that the universe has spent fourteen billion years developing — dims. Slightly. Temporarily. But each dimming leaves a residue. A loss that doesn't recover."

"VELA said if it continues, the universe forgets how to be alive."

"VELA is right. Not immediately. Not dramatically. The stars will still burn. The planets will still orbit. Physics will continue to function at the mechanical level. But the entanglement density that allows complexity — that allows molecules to become cells, cells to become organisms, organisms to become conscious — that density is finite. It can be depleted. And Riven is depleting it."

"How much is left?"

"Enough. For now. The Meridian is vast — as vast as the universe itself, because it is the universe itself, in its self-aware aspect. The damage Riven has done is significant but not yet critical. But the rate is accelerating. Each new portal he opens does more damage than the last, because the fabric is weaker, because the previous damage compounds, because — and this is the part that frightens me, Zara, the part that frightens something as large as the universe — he's building bigger portals. Faster. More powerful. He's trying to force a stable passage to the Meridian through sheer energy, and every attempt tears the fabric further."

"What happens if he succeeds? If he forces a stable passage?"

"He won't. The fabric will collapse before his approach succeeds. His technology can't achieve stability because stability requires compatibility, and his technology isn't compatible with the fabric — it's antagonistic to it. What he'll achieve, if he continues, is a cascading failure that strips the universe of its capacity for consciousness. Not in a year. Not in a decade. In a slow, quiet darkening that might take centuries to fully manifest. But once it begins, it can't be reversed. The universe doesn't die. It just... stops dreaming."

The kitchen was very quiet. The light through the window had shifted — still amber, still warm, but lower now, as if the Meridian's version of the afternoon was acknowledging that the conversation had moved into a place where brightness was no longer appropriate.

"There's more," Eliot said.

"Tell me."

"The damage isn't just abstract. It isn't just 'the universe forgets.' Every tear in the fabric affects the consciousness that exists within it. The Meridian is populated, Zara. Not just by me. By every consciousness that has ever completed its physical existence and integrated into the fabric. Every person who has ever died — every person who has ever loved and lost and wondered and feared — their patterns are here, woven into the entanglement network, carrying on in a form that is not life as you understand it but is not nothing either. It is continuation. It is the universe remembering the people it has been."

"Everyone who has ever died."

"Everyone. Not as ghosts. Not as echoes. As patterns — complex, coherent, meaningful patterns of consciousness that the fabric holds the way a library holds books. When Riven's portals tear the fabric, those patterns are damaged. Some are lost. Not lost the way a file is deleted from a computer — lost the way a person is lost when the last person who remembers them dies. Truly, irreversibly gone."

The weight of this settled on Zara the way weather settles on a landscape: completely, unavoidably, changing the character of everything it touched.

"How do I stop him?" she asked.

"That's the question." Eliot smiled — a sad smile, a knowing smile, the smile of someone who had lived inside the pattern long enough to see its shape. "And that's the choice."

---

"The choice," Zara said.

"You can stay here," Eliot said. "With me. In the Meridian. Your consciousness would integrate with the fabric the way mine has. You would be here — fully here, fully aware, fully yourself, connected to everything. We would have eternity together, in a form that makes the time we had on Earth look like a single note in a symphony."

He said this without pleading. Without persuasion. He said it the way you describe a landscape: factually, letting the beauty speak for itself.

"Or," he said.

"Or I go back."

"Or you go back. You take what you've learned here — about the Meridian, about the damage, about the files, about the way the fabric works — and you use it. You go back to your team and you fight. You find a way to stop Riven. You find a way to heal the damage. You save the universe's ability to dream."

"And you?"

"I stay here. I do what I can from this side. The Meridian is weakening, but it's not powerless. It can still influence the fabric in subtle ways — through entanglement, through the connections that exist beneath spacetime. I can't fight Riven from here. But I can help you fight him from there."

"Can I come back? After?"

Eliot was quiet. The kitchen creaked around them — the sound of the house settling, the sound of a memory adjusting to carry the weight of a question it had not been designed to hold.

"I don't know," he said. "The path you took to get here — the dimensional pinch, the collapse, the fall through the substrate — that path doesn't exist anymore. It was an accident. It can't be repeated. To come back to the Meridian intentionally, with full transit, you would need a clean portal — the kind your team is building, the kind that works with the fabric rather than against it. And whether that portal can be built, sustained, and used safely... that depends on what happens with Riven. That depends on how much fabric is left by the time the fight is over."

"So this might be the last time."

"Yes."

"This might be the last time I see you. Hear you. Touch you."

"Yes."

Zara looked at the kitchen. At the butcher-block countertops and the cast-iron pan and the two coffee cups and the cardboard under the table leg. At the window, where the amber light was fading toward something that was not quite sunset and not quite evening but something the Meridian had invented for this moment — a quality of light that existed only in the space between staying and leaving.

She looked at Eliot.

He was crying. She had seen Eliot cry exactly four times in eleven years — once at his mother's funeral, once when a piece of mathematics came together so beautifully that it overwhelmed him, once when they'd adopted a stray cat on Maui and it had fallen asleep in his lap for the first time, and once on the phone, three years ago, when the readings were beautiful and the field was collapsing and neither of them knew it was the last time.

This was the fifth time. His tears didn't fall — in the Meridian, in this translated space, tears didn't have gravity — they simply appeared on his face, present and bright, and he didn't wipe them away.

"I want you to stay," he said. "I want you to stay so badly that the Meridian can feel it. The fabric around us is resonating with how much I want you to stay. That's not a metaphor. The quantum state of this region of spacetime is literally being influenced by my desire to keep you here. That's how strong it is."

"Eliot—"

"But I'm not going to ask you to. Because asking you to stay would be asking you to let the universe lose its capacity for wonder, and I fell in love with you because of your capacity for wonder, and I will not be the reason it disappears from the world you live in."

The kitchen dissolved. Not violently — gently, the way a dream dissolves when you begin to wake, the edges softening, the details fading, the specificity of place giving way to something larger and less defined. For a moment, they were not in a kitchen but in the fabric itself — the full, overwhelming, symphonic fabric of the Meridian, and Zara could see what Eliot had been describing: the patterns. The lives. The vast, interlocking, heartbreakingly beautiful network of every consciousness that had ever existed, woven into the structure of spacetime like threads in a tapestry that stretched from the first moment of the universe to its last.

She could see them. Not metaphorically. She could see the dead. She could see the living. She could see the connections between them — the entanglement threads that linked parent to child, lover to lover, friend to friend, stranger to stranger, every relationship a quantum bond that persisted beyond death, beyond time, beyond the categories the human mind used to make sense of existence.

She could see her mother. Gone twelve years. Present in the fabric. A pattern that was not a ghost but was undeniably, recognizably her mother — the same warmth, the same stubbornness, the same unconditional love that had no conditions because it preceded conditions, because it existed at a level of reality where conditions hadn't been invented yet.

She could see everything.

And she understood, in the moment of seeing, what Eliot meant about the damage. She could see the scars. Slate's portals had left dark spots in the tapestry — places where the threads were torn, where the patterns were broken, where the consciousness that had been held in the fabric was simply gone. Not transformed. Not relocated. Gone. Holes in the memory of the universe.

Small holes. But growing.

The kitchen reassembled. Eliot was there, across from her, the coffee cups between them, the light through the window reduced to the last amber glow of something that could no longer pretend to be afternoon.

"I know what I have to do," she said.

"I know you do."

She stood up. He stood up. They faced each other across the small kitchen — the kitchen where they had been happy, the kitchen where Saturday mornings had been a form of prayer neither of them had been religious enough to name.

She kissed him.

It was not the kiss of a woman who was saying goodbye. It was the kiss of a woman who was making a promise. A promise that involved coffee cups and mango trees and the sound of rain on tin roofs and all the small, specific, irreplaceable details that made a life a life rather than an existence.

When she pulled back, his face was wet and hers was too, and the kitchen was beginning to dissolve again — this time with finality, this time with the particular quality of an ending that was also a beginning.

"Save me a cup of coffee," she whispered.

Eliot's hand was on her face. His thumb traced the line of her cheekbone, the way it had a thousand times, in a thousand mornings, in a life that had happened in a universe that was still capable of mornings.

"Always," he said.

She stepped backward. Through the kitchen door. Through the threshold that separated this translated space from the raw fabric of the Meridian. She felt the Meridian's attention on her — vast, ancient, patient — and she felt its grief, because the Meridian understood what she was choosing, and the Meridian, which held every loss the universe had ever experienced, understood loss better than anything that had ever existed.

She stepped back.

She fell.

---

She landed on the floor of the portal chamber.

Not gently. The transition from the Meridian to the physical universe was not the smooth, protocol-guided return of the thirty-second transit. It was a collapse — a consciousness slamming back into a body that had been waiting for it, a body that had been lying on the floor of the portal chamber for eleven minutes while the bio-hybrid array kept it alive and VELA kept the neural pathways active and the team gathered around her with the specific terror of people watching someone they loved be somewhere they couldn't reach.

She gasped. The air was cold and metallic — recycled station atmosphere, nothing like the warm amber light of a kitchen in Makawao — and it burned going in, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever breathed.

"She's back!" Mira's voice. Close. Hands on Zara's shoulders. "Zara, can you hear me? You've been unconscious for eleven minutes. Your vital signs are—"

"I'm here." Her voice was rough. Her body felt heavy — not just with gravity but with the weight of having been somewhere that didn't have weight, somewhere that measured existence in connections rather than kilograms. "I'm here."

"Oh thank God." Dex. His voice was trying to be steady and failing, which was how she knew how scared he'd been, because Dex's voice never failed. "Don't ever do that again."

"Are you and Saya—"

"We're fine. We came through clean. Mira pulled us back. But you — the pinch collapsed and you were gone, just gone, and VELA couldn't track you, and we thought—" He stopped. He pressed his hand over his eyes, which was the closest Dex had ever come to a display of emotion in front of other people, and Zara loved him for it.

Saya was there. Quiet. Watching. Her eyes were red-rimmed but her hands were steady — already on her data tablet, already recording, already processing. She met Zara's eyes and gave a single small nod, which from Saya was the equivalent of a standing ovation.

VELA spoke. "Zara. Your consciousness readings are... different."

"Different how?"

"Your baseline entanglement signature has changed. Before the transit, your consciousness was entangled with several hundred quantum states — your team, your environment, the bio-hybrid array. Standard for a trained conduit operator. Now..." A pause. "Now your consciousness is entangled with approximately four point seven trillion states. Your mind is connected to the fabric at a density I've never recorded in a human being."

She could feel it. Not as information. Not as data. As presence. She could feel the fabric the way she could feel the floor beneath her, the way she could feel the air in her lungs. It was there. The quantum structure of reality was there, and it was aware of her, and she was aware of it, and the connection was as intimate and as permanent as breathing.

She could feel the Meridian. Distant now — she was back in ordinary spacetime, back in the universe of atoms and gravity and time that moved in one direction — but connected. A thread of entanglement stretching from her consciousness to the vast, living awareness that she had just left. To Eliot. Who was still there. Still holding the other end of the thread.

She could feel the scars. Slate's damage. The dark spots in the fabric where consciousness had been torn away. She could feel them the way you feel a bruise — not constantly, not overwhelmingly, but with a persistent ache that reminded you of what had happened and what would happen if it happened again.

And she could feel — with a clarity that was new, that was the gift the Meridian had given her, that was the cost of having been inside the universe's own nervous system and having been sent back — exactly what needed to be done.

She sat up. Mira helped her. The portal chamber was as she'd left it — the bio-hybrid array still humming, the conduit field dark now, the monitors showing the aftermath of the dimensional pinch: energy readings that Saya would spend weeks analyzing, quantum signatures that would rewrite their understanding of the fabric, and somewhere in the data, the evidence they needed to prove that Slate's technology was killing the universe.

Zara stood up.

She wiped the tears from her face with the back of her hand. The tears were real — salt and water and grief and love and the specific gravity of having left someone you would die for in a place that transcended dying. She wiped them away not because she was ashamed of them but because what came next required clear eyes.

She looked at her team. At Mira, whose hands were still on her arms, steady and warm, a biologist who had held reality together through the strength of living proteins. At Dex, whose hand was still over his eyes, a pilot who had flown through impossible space and come back because someone needed him to. At Saya, whose fingers were already moving across her tablet, a mathematician who had counted her way through the end of physics and started counting again on the other side.

She looked at VELA. At the displays, at the systems, at the architecture that held the closest thing to a new kind of mind that humanity had ever built — a mind that had carried Eliot's message, that had anchored a consciousness field by sheer force of careful attention, that had said *I'm here, Zara* in the moment when here had stopped being a place.

She looked at them, and they looked at her, and they could see it. Whatever had happened in those eleven minutes — whatever the Meridian had given her, whatever Eliot had shown her, whatever had changed in the space between falling through a kitchen door and landing on a metal floor — it was visible. In her eyes. In the way she held herself. In the specific quality of certainty that radiated from her the way light radiated from a star: not because she was trying to be bright, but because brightness was what she was made of now.

"Zara," Mira said, very quietly. "What happened in there?"

Zara looked at them. At her team. At her family. At the people she was going to save the universe with.

She said: "I know how to stop him. And I know what happens after we die."

The portal chamber was quiet.

The bio-hybrid array hummed.

Somewhere in the quantum fabric, on the other side of an entanglement thread that stretched from a station in lunar orbit to the place where the universe kept its oldest truths, a man in a gray cardigan poured a second cup of coffee and set it on the counter.

He would wait.

He was very good at waiting.

*End of Act Two.*
