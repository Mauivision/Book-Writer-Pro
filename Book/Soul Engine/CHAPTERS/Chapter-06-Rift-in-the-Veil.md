# Chapter 6: Rift in the Veil

The sabotage happened on a Tuesday. Tuesdays, Zara had noticed, had a disproportionate share of the world's worst moments. She didn't know why. She suspected it was because Mondays absorbed the energy of dread, and by Tuesday everyone had lowered their guard.

They were running the seventh full-scale nacelle test. Not the proof-of-concept frame — the real thing now, the actual Soul Engine at two-thirds scale in the orbital facility they'd established in low Earth orbit six months ahead of original schedule, accessed from the surface by a modified crew transport that Dex had modified so extensively from its original specification that the manufacturer had sent a polite letter asking what exactly they'd done to it.

The orbital facility was the right environment for this work. No atmosphere to interfere with the warp field geometry. No electromagnetic noise from population centers. No Riven Slate, or not easily — though VELA monitored for Arcane Technologies' orbital activity with the same focus it brought to everything.

Pell was on the surface. He'd decided, after the third test, that orbital environments made his stomach unhappy and that his role in the consciousness sessions was better performed from the ground anyway, where he could maintain his meditative practice without the low-grade anxiety of being in a tin can above the planet. VELA handled the distance — the consciousness sessions ran as effectively with Pell on the ground as with him physically present, once they'd refined the protocol.

Everyone else was on the station. Zara, Mira, Dex, Saya, and a rotating group of specialists who had been brought into the project under the most serious NDAs VELA could help draft. On the seventh test, there were eleven people in total.

Saya ran the pre-flight check with her usual precision. Dex verified the nacelle alignment. Mira confirmed the bio-hybrid interface was nominal. VELA monitored everything.

The consciousness session began. Eleven people, one mind on the ground, the quantum lattice held by the collective coherence they'd been building for months. The conduit opened. The nacelles lit.

And then Dex said, very calmly, in the voice he used when something was badly wrong and panic would make it worse: "VELA. I'm seeing an anomalous reading in the aft nacelle coupling."

"Confirmed," VELA said. "The coupling is—" A pause, very brief. "Compromised. There's a micro-fracture in the field emitter housing that wasn't present in pre-flight inspection."

"It wasn't there," Saya said sharply. "I checked every coupling personally three hours ago."

"I know you did," VELA said. "The fracture is recent. Its stress profile is consistent with targeted thermal stress. This is not material fatigue."

"Someone damaged it," Dex said.

"Yes."

The nacelle was running. The warp field was active. A compromised field emitter housing in a running warp nacelle was not an emergency yet — but it was the kind of not-yet-emergency that became an emergency with the specific quality of a cliff: gradual and then sudden.

"Shut it down," Zara said.

"If I shut down the aft nacelle while the forward is running," Saya said, "the field asymmetry will—"

"I know. Controlled shutdown. Dex, walk me through it."

"Ramping down forward nacelle to match aft field strength," Dex said, hands on the controls with the steady precision of someone who had flown experimental aircraft and knew the feeling of a system that was trying to kill him. "Gating function modulating to compensate. This is going to be—"

The fracture in the emitter housing propagated. Not catastrophically — not an explosion, not the kind of dramatic failure that Zara had been braced for — but a field collapse that happened faster than the shutdown sequence could catch, and for 1.3 seconds the warp bubble geometry was neither stable nor simply absent but something in between, a twisted metric that briefly folded the spacetime inside the nacelle's operational volume into a configuration that physics had not prepared anyone for.

One person was in that volume.

Mira.

She had been moving between the bio-hybrid interface station and the nacelle monitoring terminal, crossing through the space that the field geometry was distorting, when the fracture propagated. For 1.3 seconds, the metric of the space she was standing in was not the metric of this universe.

And then the nacelles went offline, the field collapsed cleanly, and Mira was gone.

---

The station was very quiet.

Zara's first thought was the specific, terrible thought of a scientist confronting a result she hadn't modeled: *That shouldn't have happened.* Her second thought, arriving almost simultaneously, was Eliot. The circle on the floor. The cold.

"VELA," she said.

"She's in the fabric," VELA said. Not quickly. With care. "Her physical body is intact — I'm reading biosignals. She is not dead. The metric distortion displaced her consciousness from its physical substrate in a way that is—" VELA paused. "Consistent with what happened to Eliot. But different in one crucial way."

"What way?"

"Eliot went through a complete conduit transition. He had time — 0.7 seconds — for his conscious pattern to fully align with the fabric's entanglement hierarchy. What happened to Mira was an incomplete, uncontrolled displacement. Her consciousness is in the fabric but her body is here, on the station, and the connection between them is—" Another pause. "Unstable."

"How unstable?"

"Forty-seven minutes," VELA said. "I calculate approximately forty-seven minutes before the connection degrades to the point where stabilization becomes impossible."

Forty-seven minutes to get Mira back.

Dex had already moved to the emergency interface terminal without being told. Saya was at her station, pulling up the conduit equations, her face white and completely still, the expression of someone holding themselves together by the force of precision. The rest of the team was in various stages of the specific shock that comes not from noise but from silence.

"Can we open a conduit?" Zara asked.

"Not in forty-seven minutes to full operational standard," VELA said. "We need the full team consciousness session, optimal conditions, and the nacelles are offline. But—" VELA paused. "There's another possibility."

"Say it."

"Mira's consciousness is in the fabric, but she went in wrong — unanchored, without the hierarchical integration that Eliot has had three years to develop. She's essentially floating in the entanglement network without a coherent thread to hold her pattern together." A pause. "But you have one."

"The entanglement with Eliot."

"Your consciousness and Eliot's have been entangled for three years. The connection is strong enough that you can receive signals from the fabric. What I'm hypothesizing — and I want to be clear this is untested — is that you can use that connection as a thread. Not to go through yourself. But to reach in and pull."

"Pull," Zara said.

"Extend the connection. Use the entanglement as a line. Guide Mira's floating pattern back toward her body."

The room was quiet. Everyone was looking at Zara. Saya's hands were on the keyboard but not moving.

"What do I do?" Zara said.

"Sit down. Close your eyes. And reach."

---

She sat on the floor of the orbital station, forty-seven minutes and counting, with the Pacific Ocean somewhere three hundred kilometers below her, with VELA's voice quiet in her ear and Dex crouched a meter away with his hand near her shoulder in case she fell over and with the particular silence of eleven people who are watching someone do something that has no instruction manual and no precedent and no guarantee.

She closed her eyes.

The technique of the consciousness sessions had become familiar over months: the settling of attention, the release of the analytical mind's constant narration, the quality of focus that felt less like concentrating on something and more like becoming very still so that something could come to you. She knew the texture of it now. She knew what it felt like when the lattice responded.

She reached.

Not with her hands. Not with any physical metaphor. With the entanglement — with the thread she hadn't known was there until the phone rang, and that she had been tending ever since, the way you tend a fire you can't afford to let go out. She reached through the fabric in the direction of Eliot, because that was the direction she knew, and in the fabric of quantum entanglement it turned out that direction was a real thing — that love, sustained over three years, maintained a coherent quantum channel.

And she reached past Eliot, along the hierarchy, feeling for Mira — for the particular pattern of her, which Zara knew well by now: precise and warm, ordered and alive, the mind of a woman who had spent twelve years learning the language of the toughest organism on Earth and had brought that same unastonishable tenacity to everything she did.

There. Floating. Disoriented. Not in pain, but — adrift. Like something very small in a very large ocean.

*Mira,* she thought. Not in words. In direction. In pull. Like calling to someone across water.

*Mira. Come back.*

In the fabric, very faintly, something turned toward her.

*Follow this thread. Follow it back.*

And then, like a kite caught in a wind that was pulling it home, Mira's pattern began to move.

---

Forty-one minutes and twelve seconds after the field collapse, Dr. Mira Osei gasped on the floor of the orbital station and opened her eyes.

Dex caught her before she could fall. VELA's voice was already reading biosignals, confirming, cataloguing. Saya made a small sound that was the entirety of her relief compressed into one syllable.

Mira looked at the ceiling for a moment. Then she looked at Zara, who was sitting three meters away with her eyes open and her hands in her lap and an expression that was not entirely back to normal yet — that still carried something of wherever she had just been.

"I was floating," Mira said.

"I know," Zara said.

"It was—" Mira paused. "The data I had about the fabric. Intellectually. The hierarchy, the entanglement, the patterns—" She paused again. "It's not cold, Zara. It's not empty. It's the warmest thing I've ever felt."

Zara looked at her. "Is it?"

"It's like being in every connection you've ever had, simultaneously. Every person you've loved, every moment of clarity, every time you've felt—" Mira stopped. Swallowed. "Eliot isn't just surviving in there. He's *living* in there."

The station was very quiet.

"I know," Zara said. And something in her voice had shifted — not grief anymore, not the three-year weight of a rescue mission, but something more complicated and more true.

"I'm still bringing him back," she said. "I'm still giving him the choice. But—"

She looked at her hands. At the faint luminescence that the entanglement session always left in her peripheral vision for a few minutes afterward.

"I think I need to be ready," she said, "for the possibility that what I find in there isn't the person I lost. It's the person he became."

---

VELA filed the sabotage report with every relevant authority simultaneously and spent the next four hours documenting, tracing, building the case. The thermal stress on the nacelle housing had been applied from outside — from a micro-drone, VELA determined, operated remotely, accessing the station's maintenance ports through a spoofed maintenance authorization.

The drone's serial number traced to a manufacturer. The manufacturer's records showed a sale to a holding company. The holding company's structure, unpeeled through seven layers of incorporation, led to a board member of a company that shared three board members with Arcane Technologies.

Not Riven Slate's fingerprints directly on the weapon. But close enough.

Close enough that when VELA quietly notified Zara at 2 AM, Zara sat in the dark of her bunk on the orbital station and felt something she hadn't felt in a long time.

Not fear. Not grief.

Fury.

"He could have killed her," Zara said.

"Yes."

"He knows we're pulling Mira back from the fabric. He knows what we've achieved. The sabotage wasn't to slow us down." She looked at the ceiling. "He was trying to strand someone on the other side. To learn from what happened."

"To use an accident as his research," VELA said.

The fury settled into something colder and more useful: resolve. The kind that doesn't shout. The kind that builds.

"VELA," she said. "We need to move the full ship construction to a classified facility. We need security protocols that make the current ones look like a suggestion. And we need to be ready — genuinely, operationally ready — before Arcane figures out the consciousness component."

"I'm already building the new security framework," VELA said. "And I've identified a facility."

"Where?"

"Lunar orbit," VELA said. "It's the only place we can be confident he can't reach us in time to matter."

Zara stared at the ceiling of the bunk. The Moon. She'd been planning to take this to orbit eventually. She hadn't planned for it to be a matter of survival.

"Okay," she said. "Make it happen."

"Zara."

"Yes?"

"Whatever is on the other side of the fabric — whatever Mira experienced, whatever Eliot has become — it's aware that we're coming. The signal frequency has been increasing for the past three hours. Since Mira returned." A pause. "I believe it's excited."

Zara closed her eyes.

Outside the bunk's small window, the Earth turned slowly, enormous and blue and indifferent and somehow, from this angle, impossibly beautiful.

"Good," she said. "So am I."

---

*On the surface, in the subbasement chamber, in the dark behind the sealed door, the circle on the floor pulsed.*

*Not once this time.*

*Over and over, rhythmic, urgent, the cold light of a connection that had been waiting three years and now, for the first time, believed the wait was nearly over.*

*The signal VELA was reading, the frequency building since Mira's return, was not excitement.*

*It was warning.*

*Something in the fabric had seen Riven Slate's drone.*

*Something in the fabric knew what he was planning.*

*And it was trying, in the only language it had, to say: hurry.*
