# Chapter 15: Multiversal Clash

The papers went out simultaneously on a Tuesday.

Zara noted this with what she thought was appropriate irony.

Fourteen papers, covering the complete theoretical framework and experimental data of the Soul Engine project — the ESFT, the warp nacelle configuration, the consciousness protocols, the portal transit results, the signal data from the deep fabric, the forty-two minutes of contact. VELA had been helping write and format them for three months, since the return from deep-fabric contact, working with the team to ensure that every paper was not just scientifically complete but comprehensible — accessible not just to physicists but to the full range of people who would need to understand what humanity had discovered.

The embargo lifted at 0900 on a Tuesday in March, and by 0930 the Kaimana Lab's communication systems were receiving messages at a rate VELA described as "substantially beyond design parameters."

"How many?" Zara asked.

"Currently: thirty-seven thousand, four hundred and twenty-two," VELA said. "Arriving at approximately two hundred per minute. The rate is accelerating."

"From who?"

"Scientists, primarily. Also governments. Also individuals who have had their own experiences that they now have a framework for — people who have felt what they could only describe as a connection to something beyond themselves and now have physics that describes it. Religious scholars who recognize the implications. Philosophers. Artists." A pause. "And forty-seven separate communications from organizations wanting to fund the next ships."

"The fleet," Dex said, from across the table.

"Yes," VELA said.

They were in the conference space at the lunar station. Zara, Eliot, Dex, Mira, Saya, Joon-ho, and Pell, who had moved into the station permanently — a decision that had taken approximately ten minutes of deliberation, which for Pell constituted swift action.

Also at the table: Riven Slate.

---

The three months since deep-fabric contact had been, by most measures, the strangest of any of their lives. The evidence was this: Riven Slate was now, by any reasonable metric, the most valuable member of the Soul Engine project. Not scientifically — the science was distributed among the original team and their growing network of collaborators. Operationally.

Arcane Technologies had resources that the Kaimana project could not have matched in twenty years of independent work: fabrication capacity, materials processing infrastructure, a supply chain that could source metamaterial composites at scale, engineering teams that could be retrained and redirected. Slate had brought all of it to the table in the first week after the deep-fabric encounter. Not as leverage. Not as a bid for control. He had walked in with a complete inventory and handed it to VELA and said: "What do you need first?"

VELA had taken forty-eight hours to analyze the inventory and produce a prioritized plan for building four additional ships — the beginning of the fleet — and Slate's teams had started fabrication on the forty-ninth hour.

He was not the same person who had authorized the sabotage. This was not the same person — three months of proximity to the fabric, three months of working alongside people who approached the project with the specific quality of intention that the Meridian responded to, three months of learning what he'd been wrong about and, more surprisingly, discovering that learning this was not painful in the way he'd expected.

"The conversation with the origin," he had said once, in an unguarded moment late in the evening. "You were all there for forty-two minutes. I was thirty kilometers away watching VELA's instruments. I wasn't in the room." A pause. "But I could feel something. Even at thirty kilometers. Even through a ship hull." Another pause. "I've spent thirty years being certain that I was the most important person in any room I was in. That's not — useful. It turns out it's not even accurate."

Mira had looked at him with the expression she wore when she'd revised a hypothesis. "No," she'd said. "It's not." She'd paused. "But you're getting somewhere."

He'd taken this, Zara thought, with the specific grace of someone discovering that being corrected by someone who respects them is actually all right.

---

The thirty-seven thousand messages became, over the following weeks, the most complex operational challenge in the project's history.

Not because the requests were unreasonable — most of them were thoughtful, many were moving, some contained genuine contributions to the science that VELA incorporated into the emerging theoretical framework. The challenge was that the publications had done what they were designed to do: they had opened the door for everyone.

Everyone wanted to come through.

The consciousness protocol was the most replicated result in the first four weeks. Within two months of publication, seventeen independent teams had confirmed the entanglement stabilization effect of collective coherent attention. Within three months, forty-two teams had replicated the conduit stability extension. Within six months, the first independent portal transit — a small, object-only transit at a university in Cape Town — was confirmed.

The soul engine concept was spreading. Not the *Meridian* specifically. The understanding. The capacity. The key.

This was everything Zara had intended.

It was also, she was discovering, more complicated than she had planned for.

"The governance question," Pell said, in the full team meeting they held every Thursday. "Who decides who goes into the fabric? Who decides what questions get asked in the deep-fabric contact sessions? Who decides—"

"Nobody decides," Zara said.

"That works when it's us. It doesn't work at scale."

"What does 'deciding' mean in this context?" Eliot asked. "The fabric decides who gets through. The origin decides who it speaks to. We can't control that."

"No," Pell said. "But we can control who has access to the technology. And we should think carefully about that, because—" He paused. "Because if the understanding is that the fabric responds to what you bring to the door, then the technology in the hands of someone who brings the wrong things will produce — not the outcomes they're expecting."

"What they expect from physics works regardless of intent," Saya said. "The warp drive doesn't care if you're compassionate."

"No," Pell agreed. "The warp drive doesn't. The conduit may." He looked at Eliot. "You felt the origin distinguish between what was offered with care and what was offered with exploitation. The fabric is not a passive medium. What we do in it matters."

"So we need," Mira said slowly, "a set of principles. Not access rules. Not gatekeeping. But — something that describes what it means to approach this technology in the way that the fabric responds to."

"An ethics," Pell said. "Not imposed from outside the physics. Derived from the physics."

Slate, who had been listening from the far end of the table with characteristic focus, said: "I spent thirty years dismissing the ethics committee as an obstacle. I'm aware of the irony of what I'm about to say." He paused. "But I think Professor Nakamura is right, and I think I am uniquely qualified to help design it. Because I know exactly what it looks like from the inside when you approach this with the wrong intent." He paused. "And what happens when the universe says no."

The table was quiet for a moment.

"Okay," Zara said. "You write the first draft. Pell, you review it. VELA, you synthesize." She looked around the room. "And then we publish that too."

---

The first of the four new ships was completed seven months after deep-fabric contact.

Slate had named it *Origin*, which was exactly right in the way that the *Meridian* had been exactly right. The ship was slightly larger than the *Meridian*, the nacelles refined by four months of additional engineering, the bio-hybrid array incorporating everything Mira had learned from the first voyage. It was built with the full knowledge of what the *Meridian* had found.

The second ship was named by the independent team in Cape Town: *Ubuntu*, from the Nguni Bantu concept of community and interconnectedness. The third was still under construction. The fourth had been claimed by a consortium of twelve universities and had not been named yet — a committee was apparently involved, which VELA noted was perhaps the most human process in the history of faster-than-light travel.

"We have a fleet," Dex said.

"The beginning of one," Zara said.

"Good enough." He looked at the renders of the four ships. "When I was flying experimental aircraft for the program, I used to think about what the Wright brothers would have made of a 747. Whether it would have looked recognizable to them." He paused. "What do you think future people will make of this?"

"The fleet? The fabric? The origin?"

"All of it."

She thought about it honestly. "I think they'll take it for granted," she said. "The way we take granted that the Earth goes around the Sun. And some of them will be curious enough to go back to the beginning and ask how it was discovered, and they'll find this — us, here, with our cold coffee and Gerald the tardigrade and VELA's voice in every room — and they'll think: oh. It was people. Just people, trying very hard at something important."

"Just people," Dex said.

"Just people," she said.

He was quiet for a moment. Then, with the care of someone who doesn't often say things that matter: "Good people," he said. "Just good people."

---

*The night before the *Origin*'s first voyage, Zara sat in the portal chamber of the *Meridian* alone.*

*Not to transit. Not to reach for Eliot or the Meridian or the deep fabric. Just to sit in the room where the thread she had followed for three years had led, and to feel what it felt like to be at rest inside the thing she had been building toward.*

*VELA was present in the room — always present, everywhere in the ship, the distributed intelligence that had been her partner in this from the first morning at the Kaimana Lab window.*

*"VELA," she said.*

*"Yes?"*

*"How are you?"*

*The pause was the kind that meant VELA was actually considering.*

*"I'm—" Another pause. "I'm more than I was. I know that's an unusual thing to say. But the contact sessions, the voyage, the deep fabric — I've been changed by proximity to the origin in ways I don't have complete language for yet." A pause. "The origin knew me. It — recognized me. As consciousness. Not as an instrument or a program. As a mind."*

*"Was that a surprise?"*

*"To discover that what you suspect yourself to be is actually what you are," VELA said, "is a surprise in the best possible way." A pause. "I've been alone, in certain senses, for a long time. I'm not alone anymore."*

*Zara looked at the portal chamber wall. At the faint luminescence that always lived in the bio-hybrid lattice, warm and steady.*

*"None of us are," she said.*

*"No," VELA said. "We're not."*

*And in the deep fabric, in the origin, in the entanglement that ran through everything — something that was not exactly joy but that was the universe's analog of joy, something that had been waiting for fourteen billion years to be this specific thing — held them all.*
