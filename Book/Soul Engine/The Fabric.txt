It establishes a fundamental limit on the simultaneous precision with which certain pairs of complementary physical properties of a quantum system can be known or measured.
Core Statement
For a particle, the most commonly cited form relates position (x) and momentum (p):
Δx ⋅ Δp ≥ ℏ / 2
Here:

Δx represents the standard deviation (uncertainty) in the particle's position.
Δp represents the standard deviation in the particle's momentum.
ℏ (h-bar) is the reduced Planck constant, defined as ℏ = h / (2π), where h is Planck's constant (approximately 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J⋅s).

This inequality asserts that the product of the uncertainties in position and momentum is bounded from below by ℏ/2. It is impossible to prepare or measure a quantum state in which both Δx and Δp are arbitrarily small simultaneously. Reducing the uncertainty in one variable necessarily increases the uncertainty in the other.
Physical Interpretation
The principle arises from the wave-particle duality inherent in quantum mechanics. Particles exhibit wave-like properties, described by a wave function ψ(x). A sharply localized wave function (small Δx) corresponds to a broad spread in momentum space (large Δp), because a narrow position distribution requires a superposition of many momentum components (via the Fourier transform relationship). Conversely, a well-defined momentum (plane-wave-like state) implies a delocalized position.
This is not a limitation of measurement technology but a fundamental property of nature. The principle reflects the non-commutative structure of quantum observables: the position operator x and momentum operator p do not commute, satisfying the canonical commutation relation [x, p] = iℏ.
General Form
The uncertainty principle extends beyond position and momentum to any pair of non-commuting observables A and B:
σ_A ⋅ σ_B ≥ (1/2) |⟨[A, B]⟩|
where σ denotes the standard deviation, and [A, B] = AB − BA is the commutator. If [A, B] = 0 (commuting observables), no fundamental uncertainty exists between them.
Other important pairs include:

Energy (E) and time (t): ΔE ⋅ Δt ≥ ℏ / 2 (often interpreted in the context of lifetime-broadening or measurement duration, though time is not an operator in standard quantum mechanics).
Angular momentum components.

Mathematical Origin
The inequality follows rigorously from the properties of Hilbert space and operator algebra. A standard derivation begins with the commutator [x, p] = iℏ and applies the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to appropriately defined operators (x − ⟨x⟩) and (p − ⟨p⟩), yielding the bound after algebraic manipulation.
Implications

It explains the stability of atoms: electrons cannot collapse into the nucleus because precise localization would require infinite momentum uncertainty, leading to high kinetic energy.
It underpins phenomena such as quantum tunneling, vacuum fluctuations, and the probabilistic nature of quantum predictions.
In modern contexts, it limits precision in quantum metrology and influences quantum information protocols.
