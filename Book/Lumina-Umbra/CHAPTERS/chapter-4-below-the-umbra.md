# Chapter 4: Below the Umbra

Cael did not take her the pretty way.

The pretty way was phosphor murals and tea-room music, the Umbra that border clerks described in reports as "vibrant if unsupervised." The useful way was a sequence of heat tunnels, a ladder that had lost two rungs, and a market corridor where nobody asked an archivist why her shoes were too clean.

Liora kept pace. He gave her that much. She did not complain about the rungs. She did complain, once, when a pipe vented steam across her face.

"That's the Engine cycling the night side," he said. "It has to dump heat somewhere. Guess who gets the somewhere."

She wiped her cheek with a sleeve and did not offer a Lumen explanation for civic infrastructure. Progress.

Concord House was burned, or as good as. When they passed the turn, the door hung open and the bench lamp was a smashed star on the floor. Patrol had been thorough and fast. Reader Two was gone. The mural of the single sky had a new crack through the river.

Liora stopped. "The plate copies—"

"Cold storage. Different roof." He kept walking. "I don't put my only exit in the room with the maps."

She followed, quieter.

Nima Ro's theater sat under a collapsed choir loft, which Cael found funny in a way he did not explain. You reached it through a curtain of scavenged festival banners, then a second curtain of silence. The silence was the real door. People who talked on the stairs did not get a third curtain.

Tonight the loft held maybe forty bodies, sitting on crates and floorboards. A low stage. No screens. No cylinders. A row of children in the front, knees tucked, faces lit by a single amber well-lamp.

Nima stood at the edge of the light. She was small, fifty or sixty, hair wrapped in copper thread, hands stained with pigment. When she saw Cael she did not smile. She inventoried him, then Liora, then the satchel.

"You brought a coat from Hall Three into my house," Nima said.

"I brought a person who tried to cough a truth through Serev's plaza and nearly got filed as a distortion," Cael said. "She needs to hear how memory survives without a ledger."

Liora's chin lifted. "I need to know if Selene Kes is spoken here."

The loft shifted. Names were currency. You did not spend them loudly.

Nima's eyes narrowed, not unkind. "Sit. If you stand like an auditor, the children will perform for the enemy by accident."

Liora sat. Cael stayed on his feet, back to a pillar, because habits older than Nima's rules said exits mattered more than manners.

Nima lifted a closed fist. The children stood.

They did not sing. They recited.

Not slogans. Names.

A girl of maybe eight began, voice clear as a cracked bell. "Tomas Vell, porter, west terrace, taken in the third reclassification. Spoken by his sister, who is not in the archive."

A boy: "Rin Ade, mediant, Civil Mediation Node Four, voice removed from Record 19. Spoken by a neighbor who still has the accent."

Liora flinched at 19. Cael watched her hands lock together in her lap, archivist composure doing emergency work.

More names. Districts. Occupations. The manner of disappearance, when known: relocation, fever, harmony correction, unnamed.

Then a child near the lamp, smaller than the others, said, "Selene Kes, petition denied, still walking after the fever year. Spoken by a courier who carried her seal to the under-span and was told to forget the errand."

Liora's breath left her in a sound Cael had not heard from her yet. Not a sob. A file breaking.

Nima did not look at Liora. She looked at the children, which was the point. "Again. If a machine cannot hold it, a mouth must."

They said Selene Kes a second time, then a third. The name thickened in the loft until it was a physical thing, as real as the satchel's strap.

Cael had seen this ritual empty a room of Lumen converts and fill it with witnesses. He had not seen it hit someone who still believed, in some buried chamber, that records were a kind of love.

Liora stood too fast. "Who was the courier?"

"Dead or renamed," Nima said. "That is not the gift. The gift is that your mother is not only a sealed line on a plate. She is a sentence children can still lift."

"That's not evidence."

"It is the kind they cannot splice." Nima's voice stayed even. "Your ceremony this morning proved the other kind. Cael sent me the wrapper pattern before you arrived. Serev is not editing history in a vault. He is conducting it in public, live."

Cael caught Liora's eye. "Told you."

"You told me we would broadcast." Her voice had gone precise again, dangerous in the way of people rebuilding themselves out of procedure. "We broadcast a cough. They recited my mother in a loft. Neither opens a vault."

Nima almost smiled. "Then you understand the next problem. Proof is not the shortage. Placement is. You have a plate, a hash, a fragment the plaza heard, and a name that still lives in mouths. If you throw all of it at Lumen tomorrow, Serev will call it Umbra theater and turn the lamps hotter until this loft cooks."

Cael thought of the heat vent in the tunnel. Of the Engine dumping what Lumen would not feel.

"She wants chain of custody," he said. "I want her alive. You want a city that can say its dead."

"I want a city that can say its living," Nima corrected. "The dead are easier. They do not argue."

A runner pushed through the banners, young, rain on his collar though it had not rained. "Checkpoint sweep. West tiers closed. They took Ilen's cell off the span. Full net."

Cael's stomach went cold.

Ilen had taught him to read a plate without leaving a fingerprint in the firmware. Ilen owed him nothing and had still stored the second hash.

"Alive?" Cael asked.

The runner hesitated. "Loaded toward Lumen. Coherence wagon. No plate bag on him that I saw."

Which meant Ilen had dumped the bag, or the bag was already in a curatorial drawer, or Ilen was about to be asked questions in a room where answers were manufactured.

Liora looked at Cael, and for the first time since the checkpoint she looked like she understood the price of his exits.

"We leak now," she said, "and they use his arrest as proof we are saboteurs. We wait, and they rewrite him the way they rewrote Vael."

Nima wrapped the copper thread tighter in her hair, a thinking gesture. "Then you do not leak a file. You collect a chorus. And you do it before Serev decides this loft is a distortion that requires heat."

Cael was already at the banner, calculating routes to the coherence wagons, knowing he would not reach Ilen in time and would try anyway.

"Stay with Nima," he told Liora.

"No."

He had expected the no. He still hated how much he liked it.

"Then keep your coat buttoned and your voice down," he said. "If they take you, I do not get a second archivist who can hear a splice."

Liora glanced at the children, at the lamp, at the place in the air where her mother's name had been.

"I'm not an archivist tonight," she said.

Cael held the banner aside.

Above them, the Engine shifted the night a degree darker, as if the city had leaned in to listen.
