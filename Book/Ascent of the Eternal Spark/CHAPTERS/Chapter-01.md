# Chapter 1 — Receipts

The receipts live everywhere. They drift from the bowl by the door like pale fish bones, splay from the pocket of his coat like tongues he forgot to bite, crinkle beneath the heel of his shoe when he crosses the apartment in the grey before sun. Elias pretends not to hear them. He steps more carefully, which only makes the apartment feel smaller—as if the city has crawled in through the window to lay its own paper trail on his floor.

Coffee cools. The kettle whistle is a thin artery in the morning’s noise. Outside, the city rehearses its chorus: buses coughing, horns stitching sharp threads, a siren pulling a red seam through the air. He watches steam peel from the mug and tries to imagine it is the breath of something living. He fails. Steam curls and vanishes; breath, if it was ever there, leaves no trace.

He tells himself to take out the trash, to sort the receipts by month, to file the ones that might matter. He tells himself everything as if the voice belonged to an orderly man he might one day become. The receipts keep their own counsel. Useless purchases cataloged in thin time: pens that ran out too quickly, a magazine with glossy facades he never opened, an expensive dinner with a silence shaped like Mira sitting across from him, the afterward where she folded the napkin into a perfect triangle and left him with the bill and a look that wasn’t anger, wasn’t quite grief. A look like a door closing somewhere down the hall.

Elias swallows the first coffee, then the second, as if the heat will cauterize whatever trembles under his ribs. Thirty-eight and steady enough by most measures—project lead by title, if not in authority; competent draftsman in a firm that prefers spectacle; known at the corner shop, unknown in his own heart. The apartment is a cartography of deferred decisions: books with crisp spines, a plant that taught him neglect has seasons, a half-assembled model of a telescope he abandoned when the instructions sprouted math he didn’t trust himself to climb.

His phone blinks. Scheduler reminders. The firm wants revisions on the atrium for the Kingsley Tower; the client wants more glass, more light—an arrogance of transparency. It will face the river and throw a glittering shadow over the oldest trees in the municipal park. He pitched a design that bent the building’s height into terraces for wind and birds and rainwater, but the city committee called it “romantic,” which meant wrong. He has learned that in their lexicon romance is a sin against profit, and softness is an invitation to be remade.

He dresses as if the day requires armor. Fabric, buttons, tie—acts of small construction. On the way to the door he stops, pulled by the gravity of a frame he keeps face-down on the bookshelf. He picks it up. Mira mid-laugh, head tilted back, the salt line of a tear from wind and not from sorrow, hair lit by an afternoon they’d walked past a mural of a nebula in an alley off Seventh. The street artist had painted with his fingers, palms like galaxies, each swirl a wet prayer. Mira had said, Maybe the only faithful buildings are the ones that remind us we’re stardust arranged by a patient hand. Elias had said nothing. He remembers the way the wind pressed the smell of paint into them, and how he had thought of solvents, of cost, of surface treatments.

He sets the frame down. He leaves it face-up by mistake.

The stairwell smells of laundry and last night’s dinners. On the landing a child presses his palms to the window and laughs at a pigeon; the pigeon turns and regards the boy with a red-ringed eye that contains, for a moment, the whole city. Elias nods to the boy’s mother. Outside, the cold makes a cleaner sound. He walks the six blocks to the office. Air bites; breath ghosts; his shadow keeps up.

At the studio, the atrium glows on a wall of screens—angles that promise views nobody will have time to see. “Elias,” says Marianne, her lipstick the exact red of authority. “We need to move the structural cores. The client wants the ballroom higher. Imagine an evening on the fortieth with the river as ribbon.”

He imagines the river. In his head it’s not ribbon but a vein clogged with light, the way traffic in late afternoon stacks into luminous plaque. He nods anyway. He places his fingers on the keyboard as if they were tools and not prayers. Lines invite more lines. His hand remembers the cathedral a professor showed them in school—the way light fell as if the builders had asked permission. He draws a lobby where the ceiling rises, then erases it and draws exactly what he’s told. Bad faith has a pleasant dullness; you don’t have to listen for anything. Camus would have understood the comfort of pushing the same stone of a floor plan up the same hill of a meeting agenda, only to watch it roll back in revisions.

At noon he eats at his desk. He scrolls articles about a factory fire upriver. Occupational hazard. His father would have called it that, rolling the word around his tongue like something to chew. His father had carried steel and heat in his hands for thirty years and never learned to be tender with them. The day the line failed, the day the valve stayed open one second too long, the day everything went white then black then sirens, Elias was far away in a class on sustainable design drawing leaves into the margins of a lecture about costs. It took three trains and a cab to reach the hospital. He remembers the smell of burned fabric, the way a nurse’s shoes squeaked, the way his father’s chest rose like a machine and fell like a man.

“Don’t be foolish,” his father had said the last time they’d stood by the river, long before the fire. “The world eats men who dream too long. Build something that holds.” Elias had said he would. He did not say what. He did not ask what it meant to hold.

A message from an unknown number interrupts: Found this, thought you might want it back. A photograph of a book—Cosmic Interconnectedness: From Stardust to Soil—his copy, spine bent, Mira’s handwriting in the margins (“love is a field,” she had written once, and he had pretended not to see). The text says: Upstairs at Voss Dome tonight. Open session. You came once, I think. The sender signs with a star emoji and the word: Lena.

He stares until the edges of the letters soften. He had gone once, years ago, when a friend dragged him to an open sky night at the university’s old observatory. An older woman with hair like snow had spoken about stellar nurseries and the grammar of light. He remembers leaving with a useful fact he employed at a dinner party and not the hum that followed him home like a shy animal. His chest tightens and—for a second—eases. He types: I’ll try.

Evening drapes itself over the glass of the Kingsley model on the conference table. He lingers after everyone else leaves, moving around the tower as if it were a single word he can’t pronounce. The model’s reflection sits in the window, his own face hovering behind it, superimposed on geographies he once believed he would arrange to be merciful. He collects his things. On the sidewalk the city’s breath is warmer. He follows the long spine of Seventh toward the dome, past the mural—its colors darker now, blues that you have to lean into, a nebula that looks like a wound healing. The street artist is there, fingertips stained, adding a faint wash. “It keeps changing,” Elias says, more to the sky than to the man. “It always was,” the man says, not looking at him. Wind lifts the scent of turpentine and something sweet beneath it.

The dome is old enough to creak. Inside, seats are arranged in a circle as if the sky were something a room could ask to come closer. Dr. Lena Voss stands by the instrument like a captain beside a benign engine. “Stellar births,” she says when they’ve settled. “We are witnessing what is always happening, which is to say, we are learning to call the obvious divine.” She speaks of gravity as a kindness that keeps quarrelsome matter from flying apart. She speaks of death as another word for transformation that isn’t ours to fear. Elias feels his shoulders drop, then stiffen at the thought that this softness is a trick.

When they dim the lights, the ceiling opens into a sky he has avoided his whole life by looking down. He sees the shoulder of Orion like a question a father might ask and a son might finally be able to answer. He sees dark lanes where dust eats light and remembers how grief works. He sees a spray of meteors—just a recorded show, he knows—but each cutting line is a sentence in a language his body surprises him by understanding.

On the walk home, the wind leans on him sweetly, like an apology he hasn’t earned. The receipts in his pocket make a dry whisper with each step. He thinks of fire escapes, of ropes, of ladders with missing rungs. He crosses the bridge that cuts the river’s dark ribbon. Below, water moves like a beast turning in sleep. The railing is cold. He presses his palms to metal and feels the pulse in his wrists as if it belonged to the city, as if it belonged to something older.

He tells himself he will throw away the receipts when he gets home. He tells himself tomorrow he will draw a building that asks permission. He tells himself he will text Mira and not about anything easy. He tells himself he will go back to the dome. He tells himself stories and, for once, believes they are not lies.

At his building, he climbs the stairs slower. He opens the door to the apartment and the city does not follow him in. In the quiet it is easier to hear the faintest hum—a sound like the universe whispering through a seashell pressed to his ear. He looks at the frame on the shelf, still face-up, and the laughter in it seems to be not about him but with him.

He moves through the rooms collecting paper in his hands. He does not throw them out. He stacks them in a neat pile on the table, a small cairn of his days. He stands before it, breathes, and feels the weight of the world like a coat he is finally allowed to remove. Outside, a star peeks through a wound in the city’s cloud, and for a moment, the wound does not close.
