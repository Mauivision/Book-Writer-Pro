# Chapter 8 — Voss Dome

The dome smells like old wood and newer paint and the particular silence that accumulates when people gather to look up. Dr. Lena Voss stands by the instrument, her hands resting on the eyepiece as if it were a shoulder she's been waiting to comfort. Her hair is a cloud of white, and when she moves, it catches the dim light like a nebula itself.

"Welcome back," she says, not turning. "I wondered if you would come."

Elias takes a seat in the circle. There are others—a woman with a notebook, a man with a telescope case, a teenager who keeps checking her phone and then putting it away as if the habit embarrassed her. The dome creaks, a sound like a ship remembering it's a ship.

"Tonight," Dr. Voss says, "we're going to talk about stellar births. Not because birth is always gentle, but because it's always necessary." She touches a lever and the ceiling begins to open, a slow yawn revealing sky the city has mostly forgotten. The stars appear like punctuation marks in a sentence the universe has been writing for eons.

"A star is born," she continues, "when a cloud of gas and dust collapses under its own weight. Gravity pulls, heat builds, pressure mounts. At the center, fusion begins—hydrogen becomes helium, and in that transformation, light is born. But here's the thing: the star doesn't just appear. It comes from something that had to die first."

She moves to a screen, taps it, and an image blooms: a stellar nursery, wisps of color where new stars are forming. "This," she says, "is the Carina Nebula. In it, stars are being born from the remnants of stars that died. The death feeds the birth. The cycle is not a circle but a spiral, always moving forward, always learning."

Elias feels something in his chest loosen. He thinks of his father, of the factory, of the way death and birth had seemed like enemies and not like partners in a dance he was only beginning to understand.

"And when a star dies," Dr. Voss says, "it doesn't disappear. It explodes—a supernova—and in that explosion, it creates the elements that will become planets, moons, us. Your body contains atoms that were forged in the heart of a star that died billions of years ago. You are literally stardust, and you are literally carrying the memory of a star's death in every cell."

The dome fills with a hum that might be machinery or might be something else. Elias closes his eyes and imagines the atoms in his hands, in his heart, in his bones. They remember fire. They remember transformation. They remember that death is not an end but a becoming.

"Personal rebirth," Dr. Voss says, as if reading his thoughts, "works the same way. Something has to collapse—an old way of being, a belief that no longer serves, a version of yourself that's grown too heavy. The collapse is painful, yes. But it's also necessary. And from it, new light can be born."

She looks at him directly now, and her eyes hold the same kindness the stars seem to hold when you look at them long enough. "You've been carrying a lot of weight, Elias. You don't have to let it kill you. You can let it transform you instead."

The session ends. People file out. Elias lingers. Dr. Voss adjusts the telescope and says, "Want to look?"

He does. Through the eyepiece, a star resolves into a point of light that seems to pulse, as if it were breathing. Or maybe that's just his own pulse, his own breath, synchronized with something older than both.

"What do you see?" she asks.

"A beginning," he says. "And an ending. Both at the same time."

She smiles. "That's what everything is. Always."
