# Chapter 11 — Entropy and Impermanence

The book does not heal him. It does something truer: it makes the wound legible. Elias runs his finger under a paragraph about entropy, about order courting disorder with the inevitability of winter, and feels a tenderness for mugs chipped and sweaters pilled, for cities that shed their gloss at the edges where real life rubs. In the dome, Dr. Voss says, “Impermanence is not a sentence; it is a door.” He writes it in the margin with a hand that tries to be neat and fails.

Later, under a sky the city forgot for a night, he watches a star die in diagrams—spectra, curves—and feels the grief arrive without the old fear’s teeth. The air on the observatory hill tastes of pine and of something colder than air should be allowed to be. He thinks of his father’s hands cooling and of how bodies obey laws that do not ask us if we consent. He thinks of Mira’s laughter bending light without trying.

He closes his eyes and the dark is not empty but full of tiny assurances. If everything tends toward unraveling, then perhaps tenderness is not an indulgence but a calibration—an attempt to align with the way the world already loosens its grip. He whispers, not to anyone in particular: I’m listening.
