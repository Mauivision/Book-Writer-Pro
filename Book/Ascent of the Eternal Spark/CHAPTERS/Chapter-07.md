# Chapter 7 — Streetlight Meditations

Sleep does not come clean. It arrives in fragments, each one a reel of a life he tried to leave behind. In the dream he is fifteen, standing in a hallway that smells like chalk and fear. A test paper sits on his desk like an accusation. The answer is there, written in his neighbor's hand, and he copies it because the shame of failing feels heavier than the shame of cheating. When he turns it in, the teacher's eyes hold him for a second, and in that second he knows he has chosen the wrong kind of weight.

He wakes with the metallic taste of shame on his tongue, as if his body were keeping a ledger of old sins. The clock reads 3:47. The city outside his window is a patient animal, breathing its own light. He rises, pulls on clothes that feel like armor he's outgrown, and steps into the hall.

The streetlights are still holding court. They cast pools of yellow that overlap like conversation. He walks through them, body remembering its own complaints—ribs that ache when he breathes too deep, a shoulder that carries tension like a question mark. The ache is not new; it's the grief he filed as inconvenience, the anger he stored as posture.

He stops under a lamp that makes his shadow stretch and shrink with the wind. He closes his eyes and breathes the way Dr. Voss suggested in a message: four counts in, six out, as if the body were a bellows and the air were fire. The metallic taste lessens. The rib-ache softens. The shoulder drops half an inch.

A memory surfaces, not like a stone thrown but like a fish rising to feed: his father, the day before the accident, standing in the kitchen with a mug that said World's Best Dad in fading letters. His father had looked at Elias and said, "You're going to be okay," and Elias had heard it as a command and not a blessing. Now he hears it as both.

He opens his eyes. The streetlight has gathered a halo of moths, each one a small prayer beating against the glass. He watches them and feels a tenderness that surprises him. They are trying to reach the light, and the light is not cruel; it is simply what it is, and they are simply what they are, and the reaching is not failure but a form of conversation.

He walks further. Past a park where the swings move in wind that remembers children. Past a bridge where a man sleeps under a blanket that used to be a curtain. Past a mural that has grown again—now there are stars inside the nebula, pinpricks of white that look like wishes made solid.

At home, he sits on the floor and writes in a journal he bought three weeks ago and left untouched. The words come like water from a tap that's been off too long—first rusty, then clear. He writes about the dream, about the taste, about the ache. He writes about the moths and the light. He writes: Shame is not a stain but a color that can be mixed with others. Red for rage, yes, but also gold for forgiveness, blue for curiosity about what comes next.

He writes until his hand cramps and the sky outside begins to remember its name. Then he sleeps, and this time the dreams are not reels but photographs left in the sun, fading toward something lighter.
