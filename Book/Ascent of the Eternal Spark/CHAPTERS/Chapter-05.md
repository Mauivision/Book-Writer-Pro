# Chapter 5 — Collapse Under Neon

It happens on a Tuesday that wears a Thursday’s exhaustion. The office spit him out at ten. He ate a carton of noodles as if the fork could keep time. He walked to make the thoughts sleep. The city obliged with light.

Seventh Avenue insisted on itself. Screens stitched narratives he did not consent to. A bus sighed like a horse that remembers fields. Elias stopped at a crosswalk that did not notice him and the signal changed and changed back and he did not move because the wind had reached him first—a hand on the back, a gentle push the way a friend nudges you toward a door you’ll be glad you opened.

His knees went first, then the air left. He leaned against a lamppost and let the pole hold him the way a tree might hold a climbing child. His chest practiced the old trick—tighten, release, forget—and got it wrong. Sound became cotton. Then glass. Then space.

He tilted his head. Between two towers, a gap, and in the gap a star, arrogant and small, punching a pin through the city’s careful dark. It wasn’t alone. A second. A third. The building edges made a frame and the frame made a truth: there was a ceiling beyond the ceiling. The hum from the dome returned, a low animal that had learned his name.

From a bench, a woman asked, “Are you okay?” Her voice was a bridge. He crossed it. “Yes,” he said, and the yes felt like a first draft. He sat. Breath returned in little negotiations. The neon’s buzz softened into a cousin of bees. A siren passed and did not claim him.

He stayed until the gap filled with clouds and then emptied again and learned that even the city allowed the sky its sentences. He stood and the world did not tilt. He took the long way home. On the bridge, wind braided itself through the rails and into his sleeves. He placed his hand on the cold and felt a rhythm older than him keeping time inside him.

At his desk he opened the sketchbook to the page he had been afraid to deserve. He drew a terrace that would hold rain and birds and people who had forgotten that water could be a teacher. He wrote in the margin in a hand that surprised him with its clarity: Start by asking what must be light enough to rise.

Then he slept without the fan on, and in the dream he did not fly; he floated, weight rearranged, as if a hand had come from below and said, You can set some of this down.
