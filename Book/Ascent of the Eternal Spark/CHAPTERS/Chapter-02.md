# Chapter 2 — Load-bearing Walls

On the forty-second floor the model looks like a promise and a threat. The atrium’s spine gleams, a vertebrae of glass that will pour light down a lobby designed to be photographed more than lived in. Elias aligns two transparent panels, and in the panels, the city imitates a river it helped to choke.

“More exposure,” Marianne says, tapping the mezzanine with a nail. “We want the lobby to feel like morning, all day.” Exposure: the word photographers use and the word for wound. Elias nods, sketches a cut, and feels a draft where there are no windows yet.

Emails bloom and die. The client wants to see versions where the park below is framed as an amenity, not a responsibility. Engineers send a note about wind shear. Marketing requests a phrase to sell the atrium’s feeling: river of light, breath of the city, cathedral of glass. He writes none of them and deletes all three.

By mid-afternoon he escapes down the stairs, a pen still behind his ear like a flag of truce. Outside the building site, a barricade fences off a stand of old trees. Their trunks hold the language of seasons, rings counting debts. He rests his palm on bark and feels the vibration of trucks moving soil as if the earth were purring to keep itself calm.

A kid with a skateboard stops. “They gonna cut those?”

“Not if we can help it,” Elias says, hearing the lie and wanting it to be a vow. The kid rides off, the sound of wheels a quick prayer over concrete.

Back at his desk, he draws a load path that would allow a terrace where birds might remember their routes. He tucks the sketch into a folder labeled Alternatives as if hiding hope could make it ripen. The firm’s server groans. Meetings calendar themselves. When the conference room fills, he walks them through versions that behave. He swallows the sentence that would have asked permission of the sky.

After, he sits alone with the model, the city outside turning copper at the edges. He imagines the building as a body and asks where its heart is. The answer, if there is one, is not a place but a weight. He thinks of his father’s word hold and feels how often he has designed for gravity and not for mercy.

On his way home, he passes the alley. The nebula mural has grown. A band of violet now embraces the blue, a tenderness painted into brick. The street artist wipes his hands on a rag the color of dusk. Elias almost says, How do you know when it’s finished? But the artist is humming—some small, private gravity—and Elias understands that sometimes the question is a theft.

He walks. He rehearses a sentence he might send to Mira. He imagines the building asking, softly, to be lighter. In his pocket, a receipt sharpens a corner and he presses a thumb to it until the paper learns his pulse.
